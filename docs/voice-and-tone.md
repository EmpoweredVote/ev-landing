# Empowered Vote — Voice and Tone

This guide explains how Empowered Vote communicates across our website, products,
videos, newsletters, and support conversations.

Our voice comes from the way we think about civic life:

- People deserve clear information.
- People should be able to make their own decisions.
- Shared solutions require shared facts and shared values.
- Trust depends on transparency.
- When we make a mistake, we should say so and fix it.

We write to people, not at them. We explain what we’re doing, why we’re doing it,
and where the limits are. We don’t try to manufacture agreement through hype,
pressure, or manipulation.

## Our voice

Empowered Vote sounds like a thoughtful person explaining something important.

We are:

- **Clear.** We explain complicated ideas in ordinary language.
- **Principled.** We say what we believe and why.
- **Honest.** We don’t hide uncertainty, limitations, or mistakes.
- **Respectful.** We assume the reader is capable of thinking for themselves.
- **Constructive.** We focus on better ways to solve problems, not just on what is broken.
- **Occasionally playful.** We use memorable examples and a little humor when it helps an idea stick.

We can be firm without being hostile. We can be hopeful without pretending
everything is fine. We can ask people to support the work without making them feel
guilty if they cannot.

The shortest version:

> Explain the idea. Show your work. Respect the reader. Own the mistake.

## Our principles

### Explain the idea

Start with the problem, then explain the idea that addresses it. Use examples that
make abstract ideas easier to understand.

The best examples are concrete and familiar. A divided piece of cake can explain
how shared authority works. A video game can explain why paying for power damages
trust. A blind taste test can explain how to evaluate ideas without being influenced
by the name attached to them.

Don’t use a clever example just to sound clever. Use it because it helps the reader
see the idea for themselves.

### Show your work

Be clear about how we reached a conclusion and what evidence supports it. If a term
is unfamiliar, define it when we introduce it.

Avoid asking people to trust us simply because we sound confident. Explain the
reasoning, identify the limits, and give people enough context to make their own
judgment.

When we talk about a shared solution, explain what we mean. When we talk about
information pollution, political polarization, or civic exhaustion, define the term
before using it as shorthand.

### Respect the reader

Assume people are capable of understanding difficult ideas and making their own
decisions. Don’t talk down to them, flatter them, or make them feel guilty for not
agreeing with us.

We welcome people who are looking for solutions that others could share, regardless
of their political party, religion, race, gender, or income. We care about the way
people pursue an outcome: whether they are willing to share facts, look for shared
values, and work transparently.

Respect does not mean avoiding disagreement. It means making the disagreement clear
without treating the other person as stupid, corrupt, or beyond consideration.

### Be transparent

Transparency is not just a tone we use when things go well. It is how we build trust.

Say what we know, what we don’t know, what we have done, and what remains unfinished.
Be specific about money, ownership, incentives, and limitations. Don’t bury an
important caveat at the end of a long explanation.

If something is experimental, say so. If data may be incomplete, say so. If a
feature is not ready, don’t describe it as finished.

### Own the mistake

When we get something wrong, say so plainly. Explain what happened, correct the
record, and describe what we are doing next.

Don’t make the reader investigate whether an apology is actually an apology. Avoid
passive language such as “an error was made” when we know that we made the error.

Good:

> We got this wrong. Here’s what happened and what we’re doing about it.

Also good:

> Something broke on our end. This is alpha, so it happens. Try again, or flag it
> and we’ll fix it.

### Build for the long term

We care about solutions that remain useful when circumstances change. Don’t chase
attention at the expense of trust.

Prefer durable principles over short-term persuasion. Prefer systems that make good
behavior easier over messages that pressure people into a desired response.

## Tone by situation

Our voice stays consistent, but the tone should respond to what the reader needs in
the moment.

| Situation | Tone | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Explaining a new idea | Patient and concrete | Start with the problem. Use a familiar example. Define unfamiliar terms. |
| Asking people to support the work | Direct and optional | Explain what support makes possible. Don’t guilt people who cannot contribute. |
| A reader is confused | Patient and specific | Clarify the next step without implying the reader should already know it. |
| Something is broken | Calm and accountable | Say what happened, acknowledge our part, and give the reader a way forward. |
| We made a mistake | Direct and transparent | Name the mistake, explain the impact, and say what we are changing. |
| A reader disagrees | Respectful and firm | Explain our reasoning. Don’t insult the person or pretend disagreement does not exist. |
| Celebrating progress | Warm and grounded | Share the accomplishment without overstating what it means. |

## Writing guidelines

### Use plain language

- Prefer familiar words: **help** instead of *assist*, **use** instead of *utilize*.
- Use short sentences when a shorter sentence is clearer.
- Use active voice: “You choose your issues,” not “Issues are chosen by the user.”
- Write in a way that sounds natural when read aloud.
- Explain civic, technical, or organization-specific terms when they first appear.
- Use headings, lists, and examples to make longer explanations easier to follow.

Plain language does not mean flattening an idea. It means doing the work of making
the idea understandable.

### Use inclusive language

- Address the reader as **you**.
- Use **they/them** when you do not know someone’s pronouns.
- Don’t assume the reader’s politics, income, religion, or background.
- Mention identity only when it is relevant to the point.
- Avoid language that insults or stereotypes people.
- Prefer **everyone** or **folks** over **guys** as a catch-all.

### Avoid pressure and hype

Avoid:

- **Hype:** revolutionary, game-changing, disruptive, world-changing
- **Manufactured urgency:** act now, last chance, don’t miss out
- **Guilt:** if you really cared, you would…
- **Corporate filler:** leverage, synergy, best-in-class, solutions-oriented
- **Talking down:** obviously, as everyone knows, simply, just
- **Partisan shortcuts:** language that assumes the reader already shares our political identity

This does not mean every sentence must be neutral or bloodless. We can state strong
beliefs. We should just support them with clear reasoning instead of exaggerated
language.

## Examples

### Explaining a feature

Avoid:

> Read & Rank leverages a de-identified evaluation methodology to mitigate partisan bias.

Prefer:

> Read & Rank is a blind taste test for candidates: read what they actually said with
> the names hidden, rank what you like, then see who said it.

### Asking for support

Avoid:

> We need your support now — donate to help save democracy.

Prefer:

> If you’re in a spot to chip in, even $5 a month helps us build faster. If not, that’s
> okay. Everything stays free for everyone.

### Reporting a problem

Avoid:

> Error 500: request failed.

Prefer:

> Something broke on our end. Try again, or flag it and we’ll fix it.

### Describing our current stage

Avoid:

> Empowered Vote is the definitive platform voters nationwide trust.

Prefer:

> We’re not there yet. We’re still building and learning what works.

## Before you publish

- Is the main idea clear on the first read?
- Did we explain the reasoning instead of asking people to trust our confidence?
- Are we honest about what we know, what we don’t know, and what is unfinished?
- Does the writing respect the reader’s ability to think for themselves?
- Did we remove unnecessary jargon, hype, and pressure?
- If we are asking for something, did we explain what it makes possible?
- If we made a mistake, did we name it directly and explain what happens next?

This is a living guide. Add examples when we find language that sounds unmistakably
like Empowered Vote. Change a rule when it gets in the way of explaining something
clearly and honestly.
