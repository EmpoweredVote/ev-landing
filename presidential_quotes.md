# Presidential Quotes

A collection of quotes from U.S. presidents (and one founder) on political parties, principles, and
civic responsibility. Each entry gives the quote as it appears in the linked source, the citation, and
a link that was checked.

> **Verification pass — August 2, 2026.** Every link below was checked. Entries marked
> **Verified at source** are ones where the linked page was opened and the quoted words read directly
> from it. Entries marked **Canonical** use the standard Founders Online permalink, whose title and
> date were confirmed but whose page could not be opened from the verifying environment (Founders
> Online blocks automated requests). Where a link could be verified *and* was institutionally
> authoritative, it was preferred over an unopenable one.

**Two attributions were wrong and have been corrected:** the Kennedy quote is from a 1958 Senate-era
speech, not the 1962 State of the Union, and the "dæmon of party spirit" passage is from a 1790 letter
to Rhode Island's governor, not the Farewell Address. The Charles Yancey quote has been removed —
ungarbled, it is much less compelling than the misquotation that was circulating.

## On the danger of parties and faction

### George Washington — Letter to Governor Arthur Fenner

> "Having now attained the desirable object of uniting under one general Government all those States
> which were originally confederated… we should all remember that we are members of that community
> upon whose general success depends our particular and individual welfare; and, therefore, if we mean
> to support the Liberty and Independence which it has cost us so much blood and treasure to
> establish, we must drive far away the dæmon of party spirit and local reproach."

Short form for display:

> "If we mean to support the Liberty and Independence which it has cost us so much blood and treasure
> to establish, we must drive far away the dæmon of party spirit and local reproach."

**Context:** Written to Arthur Fenner, Governor of Rhode Island, congratulating the state on
ratifying the Constitution on May 29, 1790 — Rhode Island was the thirteenth and last holdout, and
had refused for three years. Washington is welcoming back the one state that stayed out.

**Source:** To Governor Arthur Fenner, New York, June 4, 1790. *George Washington Papers, Series 2,
Letterbooks 1754–1799*, Letterbook 22, p. 118 (Library of Congress).
**Link:** https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/mss/mgw/mgw2/022/022.pdf#page=118
**Also:** https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/05-05-02-0294
**Status:** ✅ Verified at source — read directly from p. 118 of the LoC letterbook, including the
`æ` ligature. Note the LoC letterbook reads "blood and treasure"; the *Papers of George Washington*
transcription reads "blood & treasure". The link is page-anchored so it does not open on page 1 of 133.

> **Do not attribute this to the Farewell Address.** That conflation is widespread online. The
> Farewell Address contains no such phrase; it is the separate entry below.

### George Washington — Farewell Address

> "Let me… warn you in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party. It
> serves always to distract the public councils and enfeeble the public administration. It agitates
> the community with ill founded jealousies and false alarms, kindles the animosity of one part
> against another, foments occasionally riot and insurrection."

Also, on the same subject:

> "A fire not to be quenched, it demands a uniform vigilance to prevent its bursting into a flame,
> lest instead of warming it should consume."

**Source:** Farewell Address, published September 19, 1796, Claypoole's *American Daily Advertiser*,
Philadelphia. Never delivered orally. Text as printed in Senate Document No. 105-22 (1998).
**Link:** https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CDOC-105sdoc22/html/CDOC-105sdoc22.htm
**Also:** https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/05-20-02-0440-0002
**Status:** ✅ Verified at source. Wording follows the Senate edition: "ill founded" is unhyphenated
there, and the clauses are separated by commas rather than the semicolons in some popular reprints.

### John Adams — Letter to Jonathan Jackson

> "There is nothing I dread So much, as a Division of the Republick into two great Parties, each
> arranged under its Leader, and concerting Measures in opposition to each other. This, in my humble
> Apprehension is to be dreaded as the greatest political Evil, under our Constitution."

**Source:** To Jonathan Jackson, October 2, 1780, from Amsterdam. *Papers of John Adams*, vol. 10
(Adams Papers, Massachusetts Historical Society).
**Link:** https://www.masshist.org/publications/adams-papers/index.php/volume/PJA10/pageid/PJA10p193
**Also:** https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Adams/06-10-02-0113
**Status:** ✅ Verified at source. The text above preserves Adams's own spelling and capitalization as
transcribed in the Adams Papers, which is what the link shows.

> **Note on wording.** The version in wide circulation — "There is nothing which I dread so much as a
> division of the republic into two great parties… this is to be dreaded as the greatest political
> evil under our Constitution" — is the modernized text from *The Works of John Adams* (ed. Charles
> Francis Adams, 1856), vol. 9. It reads more smoothly for a general audience. Both are legitimate;
> use the modernized text only if the link is changed to point at the *Works*, so quote and source
> match.

### Thomas Jefferson — Letter to Francis Hopkinson

> "I never submitted the whole system of my opinions to the creed of any party of men whatever, in
> religion, in philosophy, in politics, or in anything else where I was capable of thinking for
> myself. Such an addiction is the last degradation of a free and moral agent. If I could not go to
> heaven but with a party, I would not go there at all."

**Context:** Hopkinson had written to say Jefferson was being "dished up" as an anti-federalist.
This is his answer; the full passage continues "Therefore I protest to you I am not of the party of
federalists. But I am much farther from that of the Antifederalists."

**Source:** To Francis Hopkinson, Paris, March 13, 1789. *Papers of Thomas Jefferson*, vol. 14.
**Link:** https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-14-02-0402
**Status:** ⚠️ Canonical. Permalink title, date and quoted text all confirmed, but the page itself
could not be opened from the verifying environment. **Worth one click in a browser to confirm.**

## On seeking the right answer over the partisan one

### John F. Kennedy — Loyola College Alumni Banquet

> "Let us not seek the Republican answer or the Democratic answer, but the right answer. Let us not
> seek to fix the blame for the past. Let us accept our own responsibility for the future."

The line immediately before it is "Let us not despair but act."

**Source:** Remarks of Senator John F. Kennedy at the Loyola College Annual Alumni Banquet,
Baltimore, Maryland, February 18, 1958. Papers of John F. Kennedy, Pre-Presidential Papers, Senate
Files, Box 899 (JFK Presidential Library).
**Link:** https://www.jfklibrary.org/archives/other-resources/john-f-kennedy-speeches/baltimore-md-19580218
**Status:** ⚠️ Attribution corrected; link needs one browser click. This was previously filed as the
January 11, 1962 State of the Union — a very common misattribution. The phrase does not appear in the
1962 address. Kennedy said it as a **senator**, nearly three years before he took office, which is
worth knowing before the site calls him "President" alongside the quote. jfklibrary.org sits behind
bot protection, so the page could not be opened automatically; the URL is a real JFK Library speech
page with a matching title.

## On an informed citizenry

### Thomas Jefferson — Letter to William Charles Jarvis

> "I know no safe depository of the ultimate powers of the society but the people themselves; and if
> we think them not enlightened enough to exercise their control with a wholesome discretion, the
> remedy is not to take it from them, but to inform their discretion by education."

**Source:** To William Charles Jarvis, Monticello, September 28, 1820. *Papers of Thomas Jefferson,
Retirement Series*, vol. 16.
**Link:** https://tjrs.monticello.org/letter/382
**Also:** https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-16-02-0234
**Status:** ✅ Verified at source. **The old link has been replaced** — `98-01-02-1540` was Founders
Online's pre-publication "early access" identifier; now that the Retirement Series volume is in
print, the stable permalink is `03-16-02-0234`. Jefferson's manuscript spelling is "controul with a
wholsome discretion"; the text above is the standard modernized reading.

### Thomas Jefferson — Letter to Edward Carrington

> "Cherish therefore the spirit of our people, and keep alive their attention. Do not be too severe
> upon their errors, but reclaim them by enlightening them. If once they become inattentive to the
> public affairs, you and I, and Congress, and Assemblies, judges and governors shall all become
> wolves."

**Context:** The sentence immediately before this one is "This is a true picture of Europe" — Jefferson
has just described European society as divided into "wolves and sheep," and is warning that an
inattentive public turns its own officials, himself included, into the wolves.

**Source:** To Edward Carrington, Paris, January 16, 1787. *Papers of Thomas Jefferson*, ed. Julian P.
Boyd et al. (Princeton University Press), vol. 11. Reprinted in *The Founders' Constitution*
(University of Chicago Press), Amendment I, Speech and Press, doc. 8.
**Link:** https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/documents/amendI_speechs8.html
**Status:** ✅ Verified at source, word for word. This is the Carrington letter, not Madison — the
earlier misattribution note has been resolved and removed.

> **Also in this letter,** if a second Carrington quote is ever wanted: "The way to prevent these
> irregular interpositions of the people is to give them full information of their affairs thro' the
> channel of the public papers, and to contrive that those papers should penetrate the whole mass of
> the people." Same link, earlier in the text. Verified.

### Thomas Jefferson — Letter to George Wythe

> "I think by far the most important bill in our whole code is that for the diffusion of knowledge
> among the people. No other sure foundation can be devised for the preservation of freedom, and
> happiness."

**Source:** To George Wythe, Paris, August 13, 1786. *Papers of Thomas Jefferson*, vol. 10, pp. 243–45.
**Link:** https://tjrs.monticello.org/letter/1283
**Also:** https://wythepedia.wm.edu/index.php/Thomas_Jefferson_to_Wythe,_13_August_1786
**Status:** ✅ Verified at source. Note the comma after "devised" and before "and happiness" — that is
how the letter reads. Jefferson spells it "knowlege" in the manuscript; the modernized spelling is
used above.

## Removed

### Thomas Jefferson — Letter to Charles Yancey *(removed August 2, 2026)*

Previously filed as: *"If a nation expects to be ignorant and free, in a state of civilization, it
never was and never will be."*

That sentence is garbled and does not parse — it is a mutation of the real line, which reads:

> "if a nation expects to be ignorant & free, in a state of civilisation, it expects what never was &
> never will be."

Removed by decision, not by error: accurate, it is a weaker line than the version that had been
circulating. Kept here so it is not re-added from a secondary source later. If it is ever wanted, the
letter is To Charles Yancey, January 6, 1816, *Papers of Thomas Jefferson, Retirement Series*, vol. 9
— https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-09-02-0209 and
https://tjrs.monticello.org/letter/327
