The November test, scored

The last edition set one test and could not score it. A voter in a served area should be able to look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it. We can score it now. The 53 elections dated November 3 hold 2,235 races and 4,116 people. The Associated Press has called 508 of those races. For the 508 winners we hold a readable position for 460, a portrait for 480 and campaign-finance records for 419. 403 have all three and 27 have none of them. Across everyone who ran, 969 have all three and 1,540 have nothing.

We do well on winners because winners are mostly incumbents. 351 of the 508 are, and 350 of those 351 have a position a voter can read. Of the 157 winners who were not incumbents, 110 do. We are good at the people who already held the seat, which is the half a voter needed least on Tuesday.

Every one of the 508 calls is a federal or statewide race. They are 381 of 435 US House seats, 94 of 110 statewide executive offices and 33 of 35 US Senate seats. Not one of the 1,114 state legislative races on those ballots has a result. Neither do the 202 school board races, the 179 local races or the 103 county races. AP calls what the wires cover. Everything else needs state returns, and we have not started collecting them.

We wrote 982 stance rows this cycle and a voter can read none of them. All 982 went into draft Season 3, which now holds 2,086 rows. Season 1 and Season 2 gained nothing and lost nothing. The last answer a voter can read was written at 06:53 Pacific on October 29, so the figure at the top has not moved in eight days.

Corrections

What this edition fixes in the one before it. A published edition is never edited; the next one says what it corrects.

We said anyone could read a civic space without an account, and for three days nobody could. The Public Square shipped on November 1 and the last edition announced it. A signed-out reader sent an empty authorization header, the database API answered 401, and the visitor saw “Failed to load posts”. We reproduced it on the live site on November 4 and fixed it the same day. The check written to guard that feature could not see the fault, because it calls the database directly rather than through the client a browser uses.

The ballot figures are on a changed basis and are not comparable. The last edition counted 2,159 races and 3,900 people in those 53 elections. Today the same elections hold 2,235 races and 4,116 people, because 228 candidate rows were added this cycle. Our position count reads 1,712 against the 1,663 we published. We are not calling that growth.

The portrait licence figure does not reproduce as it was described. The last edition said 679 portraits carry no licence statement at all. Only 367 are blank. Another 159 say “unknown” and 153 say “scraped, no licence”. Those three add to exactly 679, so the number was right and the words around it were not.

Treasury’s drought broke the evening the last edition published. That edition said no Treasury tool event had fired since 09:12 Pacific on October 29, which was true when it was written. One person drilled into a budget at 19:03 that night. Another made 25 moves between 17:54 and 18:41 on November 4. We record this rather than correct it, because the claim was true and is now out of date.

We counted against a quiet database. The last stance answer was written at 15:07 Pacific on November 5. The last budget row was touched at 08:58 that morning. The nightly trivia job finished at 23:04. Nothing was in flight when we counted. That check exists because the October 20 edition counted through a load.

Budgets you can follow

  • 3,104 cities, 2,787 townships, 1,828 towns, 949 boroughs, 926 counties, 814 villages, 50 states, and 734 others.

  • Every row still carries a derivation: 244,352 published and 341,278 derived. Published means the figure appears in a document. Derived means we computed it from figures that do.

  • 24,946 rows still carry no source address a reader could open, which is a different gap from the grade.
  • The class list above is a partition, not a sample. Our refresh script complains if the seven named classes and the catch-all stop summing to the headline.

The numbers we had wrong in Treasury Tracker

Frisco’s 2018 and 2019 spending was published at about three times the truth. The city’s check register repeats the whole payment on every account line, so one payment to the water district appeared eleven times at $4,359,026.75. The test that caught it is external. A check register covers more than governmental funds, so it should sit a little above the audited annual report, and six Frisco years do. Those two sat at 3.07 and 3.00 times it. Collapsing the repeats puts both inside the band, with nothing tuned to fit.

Then a $2.52 billion phantom survived the repair. The headline figures were corrected and the drill-downs beneath them were not, because the loader had erased the key the drill-down is built from. Three years still opened to a clickable “Unknown” root holding the publisher’s own total row.

Plano’s largest payee read “MO MI” and held $483,527,652.16. The city truncates that field to five characters, so “COLLI” merged Collin County with Collin Creek and anything else starting the same way. A wrong grouping under a plausible name is worse than a missing one, so we took the vendor view down. The register now loads from the city’s own address rather than one person’s laptop. McKinney was showing 378 department names nobody published, derived years ago from invoice text. $8.1 million of technology spending was labelled risk management. They now come from the city’s own budget worksheet.

No reader caught any of this. Gates did, by comparing what we publish against the publisher’s own documents, and those gates now run on a schedule. One had been scheduled since August and could never raise an alarm, because nothing read its verdict.

Follow the money

The third leg, alongside votes and stances. The platform holds 29.2 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $8.7 billion, covering 2,285 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its filing, and superseded lines from amended filings are retired individually. Four more politicians gained finance records this cycle. A contribution does not point at a person directly. It points at a filing identity, and a second table maps that identity onto someone in our records. Following that link is what let us score the money half of the ballot test.

The purpose, mapped

An interactive Purpose mind map walks the whole vision — the thesis, and the Why, How, Who and What — with a short explainer on every concept and pillar feature.

Sources and method. App statuses come from git history across all repositories, read from each repository’s published branch rather than from a working copy. Every window carries an explicit time of day. This cycle covers the three days since the last edition was committed, from 07:32 Pacific on November 3. One repository, Fallacy Finders, has no remote, so it is the single exception and is read locally. One repository has two remotes, Civic Trivia, and the second is a contributor fork three weeks behind; the counts here come from the organisation’s own default branch. Database counts were queried live on November 6, 2026, against a database with no load in flight. The headline stance figure counts positions a voter can read. It is the newest published season’s answer per person and topic, where that answer is not a withdrawal, and a draft season is excluded because the service a voter reads excludes it. We checked that figure from outside the database, against the live answers interface. Four people were chosen so the two bases would disagree. A Pennsylvania and a Michigan legislator whose only answers are draft returned 0 each. Two officials researched weeks ago returned 41 and 15, matching our counts to the row. The ballot figures come from the 53 elections dated November 3 and the 2,235 races inside them; a person counts as covered when any published-season answer of theirs is not a withdrawal. Every called race here was called by the Associated Press and none has been certified, which is a distinction our records carry on each row. The coverage table reconciles against the stored row count, which is printed beside it, and our refresh script checks that table against the map bucket by bucket on every run. Budget provenance is read from the budget rows themselves, and the six grades sum to the total we report. Reach figures are the 30 and 90 days ending November 6, 2026, internal and test traffic excluded, each app counted once across every hostname that serves it. The hostname breakdown was re-run first, as it is every cycle, and found no new host; www.empowered.vote and read-rank-frontend.onrender.com served nobody and stay in the filter anyway. The substitution used to exclude internal traffic was controlled against the native filter on four rows and agreed exactly. “Visitors” and the per-app action counts are both unique people over the same window, so a rate between them is fair but is not a strict funnel. This edition is permanently archived at empowered.vote/briefing/2026-11-06 and will not be edited after publication. In keeping with the platform’s anti-partisan commitment, nothing in this report is colored red or blue.
