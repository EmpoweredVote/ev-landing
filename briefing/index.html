The current push: ready for November

Since June 7, nearly all effort has gone into the Inform pillar, on purpose. The midterms are nine weeks out, and the work that moved is what a voter touches in an election year: 2026 House candidate coverage in every state, audited budget pipelines in all fifty, campaign-finance records behind every federal candidate, city-by-city official seeding, and a public-meetings transcript system. The Connect pillar is parked until after November — sequencing, not abandonment.

Twenty-seven places and their counties, in four days. Treasury Tracker closed a roster it opened on the 28th: Akron, Long Beach, San Jose, Duluth, Saint Paul, Charlotte, Miami, Tallahassee, Bradenton, Palm Beach County, Macon-Bibb, Columbus and Milledgeville in Georgia, Philadelphia, State College, Fort Wayne, Gary, Columbia and Myrtle Beach in South Carolina, Nashville, Detroit, Boulder, Wichita, Aberdeen, Biloxi, Grand Forks and Lexington — 27 primary places plus 21 parent counties, 48 entities across 15 states, every one of them now carrying budget data. Some states hand over one bulk file; some had to be read an audited ACFR at a time, with opinion pages recovered by OCR wherever the page was an image — a text search on one of those reads exactly like an unaudited report. Where a year’s document does not exist, the year is named as missing rather than written as $0.

Every budget figure can now say what stands behind it. A new audit_grade axis records, per row, whether a number was read from an audited report, compiled from one, or self-reported by the government that spends the money. 28,516 of 111,776 rows now carry a grade; the other 83,260 say unknown, which means nobody has looked yet — not that the figure is weak. Brown County, South Dakota is genuinely audited but not on GAAP, and no existing value could carry that without making a false claim about its books, so a new one was added. Every loaded Harrison County, Mississippi year carries a qualified opinion on the General Fund — published, not hidden. And Minnesota’s statewide source could not be verified at all, so Duluth, Saint Paul and their two counties keep unknown rather than a grade we would have been guessing at.

The Compass learned to take your priorities with you. With an account you can now build a custom Lens — your own set of the topics you actually care about — and it travels into Essentials, so officials are compared on the parameters you picked rather than a default set. Guests are told plainly that a custom Lens will not carry. Best Match shipped alongside it, and ten topics were re-cut onto single spines so a scale means one thing end to end. The stance count on this page went down in the same motion, 33,164 to 33,038: rows the re-audit could not evidence were retired to documented blanks. The topic count rose 44 to 56.

Platform Core & Infrastructure

The shared spine every app plugs into.

ev-accounts v2.22

Auth, tiers, XP and gems, politician database, TIGER geofencing, elections. This cycle: the Compass topic re-axis — ten topics re-cut onto single spines — and the API behind user-authored Lenses, which recalibrates an answer rather than invalidating it. Carried from last cycle: North Carolina’s session-text page folds cosponsors into what reads as a primary-sponsor line, and five members across three bills were described as primary sponsors when they are not — the chairs were unaffected, but the text is voter-facing, so it was corrected. Standing from earlier cycles: the ballot a voter actually receives, seeded from each Secretary of State’s own list — which exposed federal races carrying only the major-party field, 16 U.S. House candidates where the state listed 38; Washington’s certified canvass, eight rows left open inside a mandatory recount margin; a dedupe key that matches on surname alone and shows every match to a human, because “Bob Chew” and “Robert Chew” would otherwise have stood as two people in one race; Puerto Rico end to end, parties stored verbatim in Spanish because PNP and PPD each contain both US national parties; 2026 House candidate coverage in every state; CI that refuses a migration deleting an answer without declaring what happens to its reasoning; and the occupancy-evidence view that answers “seated official or discovered name?” structurally rather than by provenance. 8,132 districts mapped, 121,178 legislative votes and 19,622 bills on record.

On the Record

Speaker-attributed, searchable transcripts of public meetings, synced to the original video. Live in the pipeline: 154 meetings, 17,077 transcript segments, 944 identified speakers, including LA City Council with 7,200 recorded council votes. Built by volunteer Chris Andrews; it feeds the platform's meetings system. It was the fastest-growing dataset on the platform through July and sat flat for four cycles while the stance work had the floor; this cycle it moved by exactly one meeting, 153 to 154. Segments and speakers did not move with it. Last cycle the segment count fell by 583 with no new ingest, most likely a reprocessing, and that has still not been confirmed — printed unexplained rather than smoothed over. The Fallacy Finders hand-off is no longer a plan: a written transcript contract, and a validator that fails loudly on a bad one.

Why the total keeps falling. A source-supports-claim check re-reads every cited source and asks whether it supports the stance we wrote. It has swept the map — Utah fell from 1,358 stances to 531 — and closed a fabricated-source retirement inside it: 298 stances retired, 160 URLs denylisted, one cluster sourced to a newspaper that does not exist. The Compass re-axis has since retired more. Retirement is not the end state: a re-research worklist feeds those seats back through sourcing, and Oregon gained 38 stances in a cycle when the platform total fell.

How a stance gets seated. A member is placed on a topic because of a bill they sponsored, read in its session-law text — not a sentence someone else wrote about them. Three rules travel with every wave: a unanimous bill cannot evidence a position; cite the session law, not the introduced bill, because four North Carolina bills had been gutted and replaced by a committee substitute long after members filed them, and a sponsor list never changes; and where a mechanism matches no chair clause by clause the answer is blank. A blank is only as strong as the search behind it: Seattle’s ten “researched blanks” rested on a single source class, and re-research seated 48 rows across 11 of 11 offices.

Two corrections to this table, both kept. Per-state counts once walked a single database link and missed everyone seeded after occupancy moved onto dated office_terms: Wisconsin read 5 researched officials where it holds 208. Reading all three links fixed that and over-corrected in the same motion, sweeping in names from campaign-finance filings that sit on placeholder offices with no geography; migration 1702 settles it — a real seat carries geography, a placeholder carries none. The durable lesson: the sum check this table relies on could not see either error, because a misattribution moves a row between buckets without changing the total.

Budgets you can follow

Treasury Tracker's database now holds budget data for 2,812 government entities across all 50 states plus DC, spanning fiscal years 1976 to 2028:

  • 2,274 cities, 433 counties, 50 states, 34 towns, and the federal budget
  • 2.6 million budget line items and 1.3 million individual transactions
  • 282,457 public salary records
  • State general funds upgraded to audited ACFR sources across all 50 states, each tie-verified to the penny; an independent blind re-derivation confirmed 149 of 151 exact matches, with the two exceptions documented
  • Los Angeles’ severed history is repaired — 22 continuous years (FY2003–2024) of all-funds actuals from one source, with no unknown rows. The defect was a label, not a number. FY2025 and FY2026 were withdrawn rather than classified, and an earlier verdict on the same data was retracted, because the tie that appeared to confirm it had been fitted rather than found
  • Every row carries an audit grade — read from an audited report, compiled from one, or self-reported by the government that spends the money. 28,516 of 111,776 rows are graded; the rest read unknown, which means nobody has checked yet
  • A fiscal year is a factual claim, and 36,311 rows were making one nobody had checked. One line of SQL wrote a literal July start onto every row it created, whatever the entity’s real calendar; making each loader state its calendar found the rest, 28,390 of them Minnesota and Ohio locals that run the calendar year by statute. No dollar figure moved, which is exactly why nothing downstream had noticed: the earlier gate validated conformity to the hardcode rather than correctness
  • Every figure durably sourced. The core promise: no best-guess data wearing a real-looking label

Follow the money

The third leg, alongside votes and stances. The platform holds 28.2 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $7.7 billion, covering 1,302 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing. The pipeline handles amended filings — superseded lines are retired individually instead of counted twice, which removed 18,553 duplicate rows and $20.2 million of phantom money. This is the “who funds whom” data behind Essentials, presented without commentary.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

Small, and not shallow

629 people used the platform in the last 90 days. That is the whole audience, across every app, over 1,972 sessions. What they do once they arrive is genuinely good — the average session runs just over seven minutes and 7 in 10 don’t bounce — so the constraint is arrival, not engagement.

The front door works, and the basis has now held for three pulls. 103 of the 312 people who reached the main site clicked through into an app — 33% in the quarter, 37% in the last 30 days. The hostname breakdown was re-run first and again surfaced no new host, so this pull can be read against the last two: the main site went 306, 316, 312. It gets re-run every cycle regardless, because a new hostname is how the next undercount arrives.

Read every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a trend line. At this volume a couple of people either side is the entire swing — and this cycle’s user-facing changes, custom Lenses and audit grades, all landed in the last four days, far too recent to have moved anything here.

A number this page published, withdrawn — and the fix

An earlier version of this section said seven people in a quarter opened any of Treasury Tracker, as 5% of 152 visitors. The rate was not sound and it is withdrawn. The events were fine; the denominator was not — Treasury pushed its in-app navigation into the URL but never captured a pageview for it, so every visitor was recorded against the bare domain. The direction was wrong too: those few produced 95 drills, 53 line-item views and 77 entity selections. The handful who reach a budget use it hard.

The fix shipped on the 16th and is capturing — budget views now arrive as their own URLs — but it is still not a publishable rate: the entities in the sample are our own onboarding checks. Treasury stays unmeasured rather than low until traffic that is not ours accumulates. The rule it bought applies to every app here: a low count is a claim about instrumentation until you have read the code path.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Grade the 83,260 budget rows still marked unknown, run the city build again on the next served community, finish the 32 North Carolina senators the majority’s bill lists could not seat, close the last 16 uncovered Washington legislators, and re-run Colorado’s legislative half once the state exposes more than the current session. Then: finish the 2026 House candidate waves, keep re-sourcing what the source-supports check retired, and land Read & Rank candidate quotes. Success in November looks like: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model actually lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not yet built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars to stand under it.
