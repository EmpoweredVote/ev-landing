The current push: ready for November

Since June 7, nearly all effort has gone into the Inform pillar, on purpose. The midterms are under three months out, and the work that moved is what a voter touches in an election year: 2026 House candidate coverage in every state, audited budget pipelines in all fifty, campaign-finance records behind every federal candidate, city-by-city official seeding, and a public-meetings transcript system. The Connect pillar is parked until after November — sequencing, not abandonment.

Puerto Rico is on the platform end to end — and the last two briefings missed it. The work landed on August 12 and went unmentioned here until now: the Governor, all 81 legislators, the Resident Commissioner, and beneath them all 78 municipios and their alcaldes. That is 161 seats, every one on a dated term and tied to its own boundary, 91 of them with portraits. The territory has no counties and no separate cities — the municipio is local government there — and the alcalde roster came from the election commission’s own certified count, cross-checked municipio by municipio against independently maintained results before a single row was written. Parties are stored verbatim in Spanish, because PNP and PPD each contain both US national parties and translating them would be a false statement about every member. Stance research there has not started, so Puerto Rico is seated but unresearched, and the map below still shows nothing for it.

Two more jurisdictions, and a pile of joins. Austin and Travis County came in at 23 seats with all 23 portraits, Indiana’s Court of Appeals is now seated by district rather than statewide, and the Kitsap and Bainbridge seats this page promised last time have their 22 portraits and first four researched chairs. Underneath: a ZIP code now resolves in the API without a street address; six office_terms rows left open-ended for officials who have since left were closed; DC’s two shadow senators were sitting on the House delegate’s district; and statewide seats were resolving twice, from two queries. The largest correction was in the money — a formatting defect in the Massachusetts campaign-finance parser had been capping every contribution at $999, because that source sends presentation text and parsing “1,000.00” silently returns 1. 11,672 rows stored $15,580 where the filings say $15.7 million; the largest real contribution, $945,000, was stored as $945. The corpus has been re-read, and every other adapter reads bare numerics and was never affected.

Platform Core & Infrastructure

The shared spine every app plugs into.

ev-accounts v2.22

Auth, tiers, XP and gems, politician database, TIGER geofencing, elections. This cycle: Puerto Rico end to end (its own government, 78 municipio boundaries verified to tile the territory with no gaps or overlaps), Austin and Travis County, Indiana’s appellate bench resolving by district, ZIP-code resolution in the API, and four join defects closed — stale open-ended terms, DC’s shadow senators on the wrong district, statewide seats resolving twice, and the $999 contribution cap. Standing from earlier cycles: 2026 House candidate coverage in every state; every California county seeded (58 of 58); CI that refuses a migration deleting an answer without declaring what happens to its reasoning, and that detects migration-number collisions; a dedup pass that refuses to merge name collisions that are provably different people, the failure mode that had already produced one fabricated mayor; and the occupancy-evidence view that answers “seated official or discovered name?” structurally rather than by provenance. The enacted 2026 district polygons are imported for TX, FL, NC, CA and OH. Underneath it all, ADR 0002 moved officeholder occupancy onto dated office_terms, so who holds a seat is a fact with a start and end date rather than a flag. 7,352 districts mapped, 121,178 legislative votes and 19,622 bills on record.

On the Record

Speaker-attributed, searchable transcripts of public meetings, synced to the original video. Live in the pipeline: 153 meetings, 17,660 transcript segments, 946 identified speakers, including LA City Council with 7,200 recorded council votes. Built by volunteer Chris Andrews; it feeds the platform's meetings system. It was the fastest-growing dataset on the platform through July, and it has now held flat for two cycles — no ingest run while the stance work had the floor. Reported plainly, because a growth claim that stops being true is exactly the kind of number this page exists to correct. The Fallacy Finders hand-off is no longer a plan: a written transcript contract between the two apps, and an offline validator that fails loudly on a bad hand-off, proven against a real transcript.

Why several states shrank. A source-supports-claim check re-reads every cited source and asks whether it actually supports the stance we wrote. It has now swept the map: Oregon lost 145 rows, Utah fell from 1,358 stances to 531 and out of the Deep tier, then California, the national set and Maryland. A fabricated-source retirement closed inside it — 298 stances retired, 160 cited URLs denylisted, one cluster sourced to a newspaper that does not exist. Retirement is not the end state: a re-research worklist feeds those seats back through sourcing, and Oregon gained 38 stances in a cycle when the platform total fell. Two cities returned nothing, which is also a finding — one of them publishes no council record at all.

How a stance gets seated. A member is placed on a topic because of a bill he sponsored, read in its session-law text — not a sentence someone else wrote about him. Washington settled three rules: a unanimous bill cannot evidence a position (a 48–0 roll call demonstrates no preference among competing values); cite the session law, not the introduced bill, because a bill can gain its substance during passage; and where a mechanism matches no chair clause by clause the answer is blank, not the closest chair. That sweep left 49 documented blanks, each with a named reason, beside 131 of 147 legislators seated. Run the other way it overturned a claim this page had published — Seattle’s “ten researched blanks” rested on one source class and re-research seated 48 rows across 11 of 11 offices. A blank is only as strong as the search behind it.

Two corrections to this table, both worth keeping. Per-state counts used to walk a single database link, so they missed everyone seeded after occupancy moved onto dated office_terms: Wisconsin read 5 researched officials where it holds 208. Reading all three jurisdiction links fixed that and over-corrected in the same motion, sweeping in names discovered in campaign-finance filings that sit on placeholder offices with no geography — Indiana then read 27 where it holds 20. Migration 1702 settles it structurally: a real seat carries geography, a placeholder carries none. The durable lesson: the sum check this table relies on could not see either error. It reconciled perfectly on both wrong versions, because a misattribution moves a row between buckets without changing the total — it catches missing rows, not misplaced ones.

Follow the money

The third leg of the transparency model, alongside votes and stances. The platform now holds 28.1 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $7.7 billion, covering 1,302 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing. The pipeline handles amended filings — when a committee refiles, superseded lines are retired individually instead of counted twice, which removed 18,553 duplicate rows and $20.2 million of phantom money — and the work has moved from backfilling to interrogating: a local DuckDB mirror for fast exploration, a settled verdict on how refunds net against contributions, and a found mechanism for the negative Schedule A rows that made some committee totals look wrong. This is the “who funds whom” data behind Essentials, presented without commentary so users draw their own conclusions.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

Small, and not shallow

570 people used the platform in the last 90 days. That is the whole audience, across every app, over 1,823 sessions. What they do once they arrive is genuinely good — the average session runs just under eight minutes and 7 in 10 don’t bounce — so the constraint is arrival, not engagement.

The front door works, and this pull found a second app being counted as two. 95 of the 299 people who reached the main site clicked through into an app — 32% in the quarter, 38% in the last 30 days. Those 299 now include alpha.empowered.vote, which serves the same landing page from a second hostname: exactly the error this page corrected for Treasury Tracker last cycle, one row further down. Every app is now counted once across every hostname that serves it, so the arrival numbers below are not comparable row-for-row with briefings before today — the main site alone reads 274 on the old basis.

Read every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a trend line. At this volume a couple of people either side is the entire swing. No work this cycle was aimed at traffic.

A number this page published, withdrawn — and the fix

An earlier version of this section said seven people in a quarter opened any of Treasury Tracker, as 5% of 152 visitors, and called it the sharpest finding on the page. The rate was not sound and it is withdrawn. The events were fine; the denominator was not. Treasury pushed its in-app navigation into the URL but never captured a pageview for it, so every visitor was recorded against the bare domain and nothing else — and the app opens on an entity picker rather than a budget, so an unknown share never had a chart to drill. That comparison reads as a verdict on the product when it is mostly a verdict on our instrumentation. The direction was wrong too: those few produced 56 drills, 29 line-item views, 41 entity selections and 13 year changes — the handful who reach a budget use it hard.

The fix shipped on the 16th and is capturing. Budget views now arrive as distinct URLs, and in the three days since, 5 of 6 Treasury visitors reached one. That is far too small to publish as a rate, and four of those six were datacenter address ranges or our own machine, so the figure stays unmeasured rather than low until real traffic accumulates — but it is now measurable, which it was not when this card was written. The rule it bought applies to every app here: a low count is a claim about instrumentation until you have read the code path.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Close the last 16 uncovered Washington legislators and fan the same build out to the rest of the state (WA-CITIES-01 in Treasury, more served communities in Essentials), finish the 2026 House candidate waves, keep re-sourcing what the source-supports check retired, land Read & Rank candidate quotes, and get Fallacy Finders v1.1 real clips flowing through On the Record. Success in November looks like: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model actually lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not yet built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars to stand under it. We are honest about this distance; it is the point of the whole journey.
