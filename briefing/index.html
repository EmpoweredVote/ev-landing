Four days of work, and the biggest piece of it is a calendar

Until this week our pages could name the winner of a state seat and say nothing about when that person arrives. We have now written a start date for 1,228 winners across eighteen states. Fifty-seven of those dates have already passed. The rest fall in December and January, and the address lookup reads them, so each new officeholder appears on the day the job begins.

The Compass went the other way. We researched 242 positions and a voter can read none of them. All 242 went into the next season of research, which is not open yet. The count of positions a voter can see is the same today as it was on Friday, to the position.

Treasury Tracker stood still. No budget filing arrived and no payment record was created. Every figure in its section is the one we published on Friday, and we would rather print that than leave the section out.

Corrections

What this edition fixes in the one before it. A published edition is never edited; the next one says what it corrects.

Friday’s edition described 4,629 answers as “finished positions waiting for the next season to open”. That count was right and the label was wrong. 346 of those answers were positions we had taken back, which are the opposite of finished ones. The season held 4,283 positions that day. It holds 4,488 today, and this edition counts the two apart everywhere it prints them.

We are also changing the basis of one figure, and the two do not compare. Friday’s edition said 956 state legislators won this month and 27 of them had a campaign-finance record. We could not reproduce the 956 from any query we can write today. On our own basis, distinct people who won a state upper or lower house race on the November ballot, the answer is 1,167 winners, of whom 35 have a record. The method note says exactly how we counted.

Platform Core & Infrastructure

The shared spine every app plugs into.

ev-accounts

Auth, tiers, XP and gems, the politician database, geofencing and elections. 37 commits, more than every other repository put together, and the seating work is most of them.

A voter can now see when a winner arrives. We wrote 1,228 future terms this week, one for each winner, across eighteen states. Massachusetts, Maine and Maryland are the three largest. Fifty-seven have already begun, 277 begin in December and 894 in January. Our address lookup reads those dates, so a new officeholder appears on the morning the job starts and nobody has to remember to do it.

The work is two thirds done by one measure and not finished by any. 155 of the 1,290 state offices that have a winner still have nobody scheduled. That figure stood at 1,286 when the work began earlier this month.

One rule in it is worth describing, because getting it wrong would have been visible. When several people win one contest, our records do not say which of them takes which seat. A winner who already holds one of those seats keeps it, and the others fill what is left. That rule applies only where the seats are genuinely interchangeable and there are exactly as many of them as there are winners. Arizona’s Corporation Commission fails that test and the rule refused it, which is the answer we wanted.

The November result file itself did not move. We still hold 1,945 winners from that ballot, and 1,943 of them are calls rather than certified results. No winner was recorded this week, and the county tier still holds four.

The stored roll-call record did not move either. It reads 11,557,651 legislative votes, the same figure as Friday and as the two editions before that. Recording who won and recording how someone voted are different jobs, and only the first one happened again.

We re-ran the contradiction check for this edition. It reports 335 groups covering 2,331 rows, against 340 groups on Friday, and nothing has adjudicated any of them. It examined 35,480 rows a voter could meet and could compare 9,278 of them. The check reports disagreement rather than error, and its own documentation says a mapping that is wrong for everyone who cites it produces no disagreement at all.

No version number is printed on this card, because nothing in the repository produces one.

Why a week of research moved the public number by nothing

The figure we lead with, the positions a voter can see, reads the same today as on Friday. It is 31,572 either way. That is not a stalled week. We wrote 242 positions, and every one of them went into the next season.

A season is a round of research with a status. Season 2 is open, so what sits in it is what the site serves. Season 3 is still being assembled, and the service a voter reads skips it entirely. We check that from outside the database rather than trusting our own query, and we did it again for this edition on seven people chosen so the two would disagree. Two whose only answers sit in the next season returned nothing. Three who gained an answer this week returned exactly what they returned before. Two long-researched senators matched us to the row.

The gap between the two readings is 4,023 positions, and it is not simply the size of the next season. It pulls in two directions. 4,314 answers are counted in the wider reading and cannot be read, while 291 are positions we have taken back in the next season that a reader can still see today. Only a query settles that, which is why we run one.

We can say what opening the season would do, because the two readings already measure it. Every finished position in it would become something a voter can read, taking the public figure past 35,000 for the first time. Nobody has set a date.

Follow the money

The third leg, alongside votes and positions. The platform holds 30.4 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $9.2 billion. They cover 2,293 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles, 1980 to 2026. Those four figures come from a totals row rewritten once a day, and the row we read was 23 hours old. The word federal is still the limit on everything this leg can do. Of the 1,167 people who won a state legislative seat this month, 35 have any campaign-finance record at all. Of the 428 US House winners, 427 do.

Reach & Engagement

The quarter is flat and the month is down
