The current push: ready for November

Since June 7, nearly all effort has gone into the Inform pillar, on purpose. The 2026 midterms are under three months out, and the work that moved is exactly what an anonymous voter touches in an election year: House candidate coverage that now spans the whole country (over 1,500 2026 candidates carry researched stances, from the biggest states down to the single-district delegations, with the enacted 2026 district maps now imported for the largest states), audited state budget pipelines that reached all 50 states, campaign-finance records that trace the money behind every federal candidate, city-by-city official seeding, and a fast-growing public-meetings transcript system. The Connect pillar apps are intentionally parked until after this push; that is sequencing, not abandonment.

The most important thing we fixed since yesterday was not a missing number but a wrong one. An audit found 89 stances whose stored position was the opposite pole from their own reasoning — the compass dot and the “why this position” text saying contradictory things about the same person. Todd Gloria, the first openly gay mayor of San Diego, was sitting at “make same-sex marriage illegal”. Mark Warner and Tim Kaine were at “end all aid to Ukraine immediately”. It was one bad batch, not scattered noise: 15 politicians hold 79 of the rows, 14 of them Maryland, and the best reading is that a research run recorded the chair as an intensity rating — 5 meaning “strongly holds this view” — rather than choosing one of five discrete policy options. It was not fixed mechanically. A blanket 5→1 flip would have been wrong, because the same batch got other topics right whenever the reasoning happened to be phrased as opposition; every row was read and moved to the least extreme option its own reasoning actually supports. A wrong position is worse than an absent one, and it is the failure this audit exists to catch.

Since the last briefing the stance total went down again — 466 fewer, the fourth consecutive briefing where this number fell — and this time the reason is the migration the last briefing promised. The fabricated-source retirement landed: 298 stances retired and 119 cited URLs denylisted, plus an entire cluster sourced to a somervillejournal.com that does not exist and cannot be re-pointed at anything (41 more URLs). Before applying it we re-read all 105 pages the sweep had left standing, which moved the count from 183 retirements to 298 — the automated check had been too generous, not too harsh. The sweep's own tooling turned out to be wrong in the opposite direction as well: re-probing the no-answer bucket found 914 of 1,025 URLs alive, invented as dead by a bug in how we called curl. Retiring good rows on the strength of a broken probe would have been the same failure wearing the opposite face, so both directions got fixed before anything was deleted.

With that sweep closed, the audit moved to its next deep-coverage state, and Maryland took this cycle's bill: 85 stances retired whose every citation was a page that never existed, 82 whose cited page never mentions the topic, 40 matched only by a site's navigation menu, 150 re-sourced to the bills they actually name, 300 repaired that pointed at a dead or wrong-person member page, and 32 surname-only rows adjudicated one at a time. Maryland ends the cycle 238 stances lighter and several hundred better cited than it started — four further passes since have re-sourced 124 generic-only rows, settled 11 more by roll call, and disposed of the pre-tenure stragglers. One of those passes overturned the pass before it: re-reading 21 rows previously judged pre-tenure found 13 of the verdicts wrong, which is the audit auditing itself and is the reason these numbers move in both directions. Restoration now runs alongside retirement, which is the part that makes this sustainable: Somerville got 32 of 62 rows back on named roll calls, Alhambra 16 of 19, Waltham 4 of 4, and Portland's re-research closed eight clusters — Oregon actually gained 38 stances this cycle while the platform total fell. Two cities returned nothing, and that is also a finding worth publishing: Carson yields 3 of 34 because its Legistar record is stale, and Lynn 0 of 30 because the city publishes no council record at all. The roster sweeps caught two fabricated people, one of them a sitting mayor.

A second correction cuts across every number on this page. The platform could not reliably tell an evidenced officeholder from a name discovered in a campaign-finance filing: of the 83,291 rows in the current-holder view, only about 5,476 rested on anything more than a placeholder — the rest were open-ended, precision-unknown terms written by a backfill onto offices with no district, chamber or city. Those records are legitimate and are never deleted, but they are not people holding seats. Until yesterday the only defence was a test on where a record came from, which caught California's discovery pool and missed Indiana's entirely. The fix (migration 1702) makes the test structural rather than provenance-based — a real seat carries geography; a placeholder carries none — and it deliberately does not key on having a campaign committee, because every incumbent seeking re-election has one; an earlier draft flagged five sitting members of Congress that way and its own verification caught it. Three figures on this page moved as a result: the curated total drops 9,063 to 8,393 and the triage pool rises to 77,002 (672 Indiana records were on the wrong side of that line), and Indiana's map tile now reads 20 researched officials rather than 27 — the seven it loses are candidates this briefing was counting as officeholders yesterday.

The headshot backlog is being worked. A list of every seated official with no portrait we can render went to the data team yesterday; 292 portraits have landed since — 158 Texas legislators, 76 Utah county, city and school-board officials, 47 California county, city and appellate officials, 5 Massachusetts legislators, and the 6 Indiana officials whose portraits actually exist. That last number is the useful one: Indiana looked like 810 missing faces and was really 140, because 674 of them were discovered candidates rather than officeholders. 1,547 seated officials still have no portrait, 57 of them on live profile pages carrying researched stances.

Additive work, in parallel: the California county wave finished — all 58 counties, Marin last — which is most of the 253 new officials added this cycle; Tarrant County, Texas was stood up from nothing (53 seats filled and 6 recorded vacant, its remaining 42 November contests seeded, five city banners wired into Essentials); the June primaries were reconciled against official canvasses in California, Texas and Los Angeles County, with Virginia, Kansas and Missouri reconciled post-primary and Washington, Arizona and Tennessee deliberately held for certification rather than guessed at. The official-URL programme closed too: every California and Oregon city and county web address is now repointed or documented, WAF blocks included, with a .gov registry built into the auditor so the next state costs less. Campaign finance reports 69 fewer federal politicians and that is dedup, not loss — duplicate finance sources now fold together instead of dropping their contributions. Officeholder coverage is now measured against the Census universe rather than against our own roster, which is the difference between knowing how much we have and knowing how much is missing.

Platform Core & Infrastructure

The shared spine every app plugs into.

ev-accounts v2.22

Auth, tiers, XP and gems, politician database, TIGER geofencing, elections. Milestones shipped all summer, including 2026 House candidate coverage waves 1 through 3 plus a nationwide sweep of the small-delegation states, so every state's House races now carry sourced stances. This cycle the core carried the whole stance audit through its noisiest stretch — the fabricated-source retirement applied, Maryland audited end to end, restorations landing beside the retirements — alongside two programmes that finished outright: every California county is now seeded (58 of 58) and every California and Oregon official web URL is repointed or documented. Three guards went in that will outlast the cycle: CI now catches migration-number collisions that git shows as nothing added; a dedup pass refuses to merge name collisions that are provably different people — the failure mode that had already produced one fabricated mayor; and an occupancy-evidence view now answers “is this a seated official or a discovered name?” in one column, structurally rather than by provenance. A roster diff built on it immediately found two seats we were showing as still held by the previous member, and a Texas senate seat was vacated after a resignation. Two research worklists were also scoped rather than guessed at: 1,641 rows nationally whose only citation is a member page, and 2,031 sourced to an encyclopedia. In earlier cycles: the enacted 2026 district polygons were imported for TX, FL, NC, CA and OH (147 of 147 rows, verification gate green); Wisconsin was stood up from scratch — state Legislature, Supreme Court, the August 11 primary, 116 legislative races, and all 17 Racine County municipalities plus the county government, its 21 supervisors and 10 circuit-court branches; Bend, Oregon and Deschutes County were deep-seeded with headshots and a sourced stance cohort; and Collin County, TX had its long tail closed. Underneath all of it, a schema change (ADR 0002) moved officeholder occupancy onto dated office_terms, so who holds a seat is now a fact with a start and end date rather than a flag. 7,082 districts mapped, 121,178 legislative votes and 19,622 bills on record.

On the Record

Speaker-attributed, searchable transcripts of public meetings, synced to the original video. Already live in the pipeline: 153 meetings, 17,660 transcript segments, 946 identified speakers, including LA City Council with 7,200 recorded council votes. Built by volunteer Chris Andrews; it now feeds the platform's meetings system. It was the fastest-growing dataset on the platform through July — 145 meetings to 153, 16,835 segments to 17,660, 926 speakers to 946, all without a single commit to its code — and it held flat this cycle, with no ingest run while the stance audit had the floor. Reported plainly because a growth claim that stops being true is exactly the kind of number this page is supposed to correct. The Fallacy Finders hand-off stopped being a plan last cycle: there is a written transcript contract between the two apps and an offline validator that fails loudly on a bad hand-off, proven against a real transcript.

Why several states shrank. We built a source-supports-claim check — it re-reads the cited source and asks whether it actually supports the stance we wrote — and pointed it at our own most-researched local coverage first. Oregon went first and lost 145 rows. Since then the check has swept the rest of the map. That bill came due at the last briefing: the platform's stance total fell by 942. Utah took the worst of it (1,358 stances down to 531, and 179 researched officials down to 48 — it drops out of the Deep tier on the map below), then California and the national set. Maryland is the current one, down 2,248 to 2,010 with roughly 600 more rows repaired or re-sourced rather than removed. A citation-level host sweep found genuinely non-resolving domains — 23 of them, not the 42 a first pass over-reported — plus one composed hostname that alone carried 57 rows, and 372 citations that were not URLs at all. Newton, Massachusetts was the starkest single case: its roster was clean, but 48 of its 55 stances cited pages that had never existed.

A correction to this table, and to the map below. Until today both counted an official's state by walking one database link — the older office_id column on the politician record. A schema change last cycle moved officeholder occupancy onto dated office_terms, and anyone seeded after that has only the new link, so the old query could not see them. Wisconsin was reading as 5 researched officials when it has 208, sitting in the palest tier on the map while being one of our better-covered states; Massachusetts read 109 when it has 302. Worse, because those officials looked office-less, their stances were being filed under “2026 candidates (no office yet)” — that bucket was overstated by 464 politicians and about 3,900 stances, and Congress understated by roughly 1,100. A second, smaller version of the same blind spot sat underneath it: 93% of office records carry no chamber, keeping their jurisdiction on the office row instead, and a query that walks office → chamber → government cannot see any of them either. That hid 29 more researched officials — 16 of them the District of Columbia, which this map was still captioning as “stance research in progress” because its count came back zero, and Indiana, which was reading 20 where it has 27. The query now reads all three links and counts only current terms. No platform total moved: the headline stance and politician counts never used that join, and the retirements described above are unaffected. What moved is which state gets the credit — so per-state figures in briefings before this one undercount the term-linked states and should not be compared row-for-row against these.

And a correction to that correction, the next day. Reading the office row rather than the chamber chain pulled in 7 Indiana records that are not officeholders at all — names discovered in candidate-committee filings, sitting on placeholder offices with no geography, which had Indiana reading 27 researched officials where it holds 20. Migration 1702's occupancy-evidence view now excludes that class from every count on this page, and the candidate bucket in the table above is where they belong. Two lessons worth keeping. A fix aimed at an undercount can produce an overcount in the same motion, and only naming the class it should have excluded catches that. And the sum check this table relies on cannot see either error: it reconciled perfectly on both wrong versions, because a misattribution moves a row between buckets without changing the total. It catches missing rows, not misplaced ones.

Two things worth saying plainly about the retirements. First, retirement is not the end state — a re-research worklist now feeds the removed seats back through sourcing, the way 18 bad Bend rows became 45 verified ones, and the first restorations have already landed. Second, the audit keeps turning up errors that were never about citations at all: chasing the Beverly Hills cluster exposed a council roster two members out of date. We would rather publish the smaller number and tell you why it moved.

Follow the money

The third leg of the transparency model, alongside votes and stances. The platform now holds 27.5 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $7.5 billion, covering 1,509 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing. The last briefing was the first to report these figures accurately — the query feeding them had been quietly timing out, so the page published 6 million and $4.7 billion while the real totals grew past four times that. The pipeline also learned to handle amended filings: when a committee refiles, the superseded lines are retired per line instead of being counted twice, a correction that removed 18,553 duplicate rows and $20.2 million of phantom money. This cycle the work moved from backfilling to interrogating the data — a local DuckDB mirror for fast exploration, a settled verdict on how refunds should net against contributions, and a discovered mechanism for the negative Schedule A rows that had made some committee totals look wrong. A six-phase deep-exploration program is planned from there. This is the "who funds whom" data behind Essentials, presented without commentary so users draw their own conclusions.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

The reach problem, stated plainly

The Compass isn't failing to convert — it's failing to get seen. Of everyone who reached it in the last 90 days, 9 in 10 engaged and more than a third completed a full calibration; that is a healthy flow. The catch is the denominator: only 85 people reached it in a quarter. The main site drew 230 unique visitors in the same window, and most never made it to the Compass. The highest-leverage work in front of us isn't rewording the calibration questions — it's getting the tool in front of the voters already in our ecosystem. The two columns disagree this time, and the 30-day one is the more current reading. Every 90-day total rose again — the quarter is still carrying an earlier surge — but on the month the Compass fell from 49 visitors to 40, completed calibrations fell from 13 to 9, and Essentials, Read & Rank and Treasury Tracker each slipped a little too. On totals this small every one of those moves is noise rather than a trend, and nothing shipped this cycle was aimed at traffic; report it in both directions or the number means nothing when it goes up. The honest summary is unchanged: the constraint is how many people arrive, not what they do when they get here.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Finish 2026 House candidate waves (wave 3 of ~4 underway, now with a live-derived queue across 178 districts in 38 states), keep seeding served communities, run the source-supports check across the whole map (the fabricated-source rows are now retired — 298 of them, not the 121 the sweep first estimated; the remaining job is re-sourcing what can be re-sourced, state by state after Maryland), land Read & Rank candidate quotes, get Fallacy Finders v1.1 real clips flowing through On the Record. Success in November looks like: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model actually lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not yet built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars to stand under it. We are honest about this distance; it is the point of the whole journey.
