The current push: four states seated, and a quiz bank read question by question

North Dakota, Kentucky, Kansas and South Dakota were seated end to end in four days — 635 offices, every one filled, every one on a dated term, and 470 new legislative district boundaries under them. Four legislatures (141, 138, 165 and 105 members), four city halls (Grand Forks, Lexington, Wichita, Aberdeen) and four county governments. Each state broke a rule the last one taught us. North Dakota’s House elects two members per district, so 48 polygons carry 94 seats and every check assuming one office per district is wrong there. Its own website publishes three member lists, and the one that looks official is cumulative — 148 names for a 141-seat body. We used the September special session’s roster instead, and swept the bad list as a control: it found all seven departures, each dated to the day.

Civic Trivia had its whole question bank read, one collection at a time. It began because Chris played the Cambridge collection and was served two questions that were practically the same. Measured on September 26, the bank held 3,855 live questions across 43 collections; 42 of the 43 have now been read question by question, and the bank stands at 3,264. What came out was not mostly duplicates. It was eight questions in one collection asking the same thing with the same answer, thirteen of another’s eighty-two about the municipal airport, a question whose correct answer was not among the four offered — twice, in two different collections — two live questions that contradicted each other, a true option scored as wrong, and, in Phoenix, a plainly wrong fact live in production. Fifty-seven cited sources had gone dead; repairing them uncovered two more wrong facts behind them.

One of those wrong facts could have cost somebody their vote. A Bloomington question told players that Monroe County, Indiana uses vote centers, so a voter may cast a ballot anywhere in the county. It does not, and they may not — and in Indiana, voting at the wrong precinct means a provisional ballot that will not count for most races. That collection carried four more wrong facts, one of them living only in an explanation while the answer above it was right, which no structural rule can see. Milwaukee, audited the same day, carried none. The difference is upstream: Milwaukee’s generator is handed a block of verified facts and Bloomington’s was not, which is the clearest lever this work has found for preventing defects rather than repairing them.

And this page’s own measurement of itself broke, by being fixed. The discovery pool — names lifted from campaign-finance filings, awaiting triage — used to sit on 76,330 placeholder offices, invented so each record had somewhere to hang. On September 24 all 76,330 were archived and retired, taking the office table from 85,134 rows to 8,804. Our published split between “curated” and “pool” was computed by asking who held a placeholder, so with the placeholders gone it answered 89,903 curated, nobody in the pool. Nothing was wrong with the data: the test had stopped discriminating because the thing it detected had been removed, and that failure reads exactly like a clean bill of health. The replacement asks what a record rests on — a seat, a candidacy, or research. The two figures above are on that new basis and are not comparable with last week’s.

Eighteen people who were not in office, and the page that counted them

A 2026 backfill gave every US Senate candidate an open-ended term on a “Candidate for U.S. Senate” office. An open term reports its holder as the current occupant for ever. So eighteen people who had lost a primary, withdrawn or simply stopped running were still recorded as sitting members of Congress — returned by address search for every address in their state, and read as incumbents by the campaign-finance matcher. Two were caught because their federal filings went dark; the other sixteen were invisible that way, because a campaign that ends without a final filing leaves a committee that still looks alive. Those sixteen were confirmed against each state’s own election authority instead, and their terms were closed.

This shows up in our own coverage table below as a loss. The US Congress + national row fell by 15 people and 153 stances, and not one stance was retired to do it — those people simply stopped counting as federal officeholders. We would rather publish a row that fell for a good reason than carry eighteen phantom senators.

What auditing a quiz bank actually turns up

Two findings are worth more than the defect counts. The first is that a citation can return HTTP 200 and be worthless in five distinct ways — a soft 404 that serves a full page, a JavaScript app carrying none of the text cited to it, a moved site, a bot wall returning 403, and a challenge page that answers 200. A link checker passes all five. One step catches all five: fetch the page and search it for the question’s own figure. One collection turned out to be cited almost entirely to pages nothing automated can read; another’s explanations carried figures present in no source at all, one contradicting a live question beside it. A supported answer is not a supported question, and the explanation is what the player reads afterwards.

The second is that a collection can be about the wrong things and pass every rule. Every check asked whether a question was wrong, duplicated, leaked or stale; none asked whether it was about civic life. One city’s bank had been generated from a department website rather than about the city — the room number of the Auditor’s office, its opening hours, the colours in its logo — with none asking which river runs through it. Another carried eighteen questions about its state inside a city collection, and ten of them were labelled with the state’s own name, which made that one visible in a database query before anybody read a word.

Follow the money

The third leg, alongside votes and stances. The platform holds 29.1 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $8.6 billion, covering 2,248 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing, and superseded lines from amended filings are retired individually rather than counted twice. The politician count nearly doubled this cycle because the matching widened, not because new filings arrived — and the same pass disputed 33 links made on a surname substring and retired the matcher behind them. A wrong link here puts someone else’s donors under a real person’s name.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

Small, and not shallow

633 people used the platform in the last 90 days, across every app, over 1,656 sessions. What they do once they arrive is good — sessions average six minutes twenty and 7 in 10 don’t bounce — so the constraint is arrival, not engagement. 98 of the 314 who reached the main site clicked through into an app, 31% in the quarter, the same share as last time on a larger base.

Read every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a trend line. At this volume a couple of people either side is the entire swing, and the quarter window slides forward, so an app can “fall” because a busy week in June rolled out of it. Nothing this cycle was aimed at arrival, and it rose anyway: over six pulls the main site reads 306, 316, 312, 297, 300, 314. The hostname map was re-run before any of these figures were pulled and found no new host, which is what makes this pull readable against the last five.

A number this page published, withdrew — and what replaced it

An earlier edition said seven people in a quarter opened any of Treasury Tracker, as 5% of 152 visitors. That rate was not sound and it stays withdrawn. The events were fine; the denominator was not — Treasury pushed its in-app navigation into the address bar without recording a page view, so every visitor was filed against the bare domain. The direction was wrong too: this quarter those few produced 126 category drills, 91 entity selections and 68 line-item views.

The fix has been recording since August 16, and the two ways of counting still disagree by nearly five times. 61 people reached a budget address; only 13 fired any past-the-front-page event. The split explains it: 47 of them landed on our own budget, one click from the donate page — a transparency visit, which selects nothing and so fires nothing. That leaves 16 who opened some other government’s budget, unchanged from 16 a week ago while the donate half grew from 41 to 47. Neither figure is published as a rate, and Treasury stays unmeasured rather than low until the half that is not ours outgrows the donate traffic. The rule it bought applies to every app here: a low count is a claim about instrumentation until you have read the code path.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Research the seats just loaded — seven states now hold full rosters and almost no stances. Finish the 2026 candidate waves, and land Read & Rank candidate quotes once the pool can carry them. Write the one trivia collection an audit cannot fix, and clear the last two collections still under the expiring-content floor. Success in November looks like one thing: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0 — whose shell, shared theme, thread links and sibling-shard view are groundwork done early. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars.
