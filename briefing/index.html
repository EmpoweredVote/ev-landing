The current push: ready for November

Since June 7, nearly all effort has gone into the Inform pillar, on purpose. The midterms are under three months out, and the work that moved is what a voter touches in an election year: 2026 House candidate coverage in every state, audited budget pipelines in all fifty, campaign-finance records behind every federal candidate, city-by-city official seeding, and a public-meetings transcript system. The Connect pillar is parked until after November — sequencing, not abandonment.

Colorado Springs, end to end in one cycle. What took months for the first city now runs as a sequence: boundaries, seats, portraits, ballot, budgets, stances. All 100 members of the Colorado General Assembly are seated, alongside Colorado Springs’ ten-member council and El Paso County’s eleven — 126 Colorado seats, 125 of them with a portrait — and both the city and the county are loaded in Treasury Tracker. Stance research followed in the same week: 77 sourced positions across 28 people, every one of the 61 quotes verified against the raw bytes of its source, and 7 of the 35 people researched producing a documented, honest zero. Colorado moves off the palest tier of the map below. One caveat kept in view: Colorado’s legislature exposes essentially only the current session — a 13-year member returns 26 bills, 25 of them from 2026 — so the legislative half is worth re-running later.

What is on the ballot is a factual claim. Seeding the ballot a Colorado Springs voter actually receives — 17 races, 56 candidates, from the Secretary of State’s own list — exposed that our existing federal races carried only the major-party field: 16 U.S. House candidates where the state lists 38, every missing one minor-party or unaffiliated. A two-name race printed for a five-name ballot is a false statement about that ballot. Washington’s was not wrong so much as unfinished: the 2026 primary field had been seeded from a pre-certification feed and marked provisional, and the certified canvass has now closed that gate — 381 rows across all 147 races — 262 advanced, 119 not nominated, 5 withdrawn. Eight rows are deliberately still open: LD 42’s second and third place are four votes apart (13,125 to 13,121), inside the mandatory hand-recount margin, so the race stays unresolved until Whatcom County recounts. The check that caught it measures the margin; a guard that only looks for an exact tie passes that race green.

Platform Core & Infrastructure

The shared spine every app plugs into.

ev-accounts v2.22

Auth, tiers, XP and gems, politician database, TIGER geofencing, elections. This cycle: Colorado Springs and El Paso County end to end — state-legislative and place boundaries, council and commissioner districts, 100 General Assembly members, 32 new portraits behind a liveness verifier that cleared 7 dead URLs — plus the Washington certification cull, and a candidate dedupe key that now matches on surname alone and shows every match to a human, because “Bob Chew” and “Robert Chew” would otherwise have stood as two people in one Senate race — the same failure mode, run the other way, that once produced a fabricated mayor. Standing from earlier cycles: Puerto Rico end to end, its own government and 78 municipio boundaries verified to tile the territory with no gaps, parties stored verbatim in Spanish because PNP and PPD each contain both US national parties; Austin and Travis County; ZIP-code resolution in the API; the $999 contribution cap closed in the Massachusetts finance parser; 2026 House candidate coverage in every state; the enacted 2026 district polygons for TX, FL, NC, CA and OH; every California county seeded; CI that refuses a migration deleting an answer without declaring what happens to its reasoning; and the occupancy-evidence view that answers “seated official or discovered name?” structurally rather than by provenance. Underneath it all, ADR 0002 moved officeholder occupancy onto dated office_terms, so who holds a seat is a fact with a start and end date rather than a flag. 7,478 districts mapped, 121,178 legislative votes and 19,622 bills on record.

On the Record

Speaker-attributed, searchable transcripts of public meetings, synced to the original video. Live in the pipeline: 153 meetings, 17,660 transcript segments, 946 identified speakers, including LA City Council with 7,200 recorded council votes. Built by volunteer Chris Andrews; it feeds the platform's meetings system. It was the fastest-growing dataset on the platform through July and has now held flat for three cycles — no ingest run while the stance work had the floor, reported plainly because a growth claim that stops being true is exactly what this page exists to correct. The Fallacy Finders hand-off is no longer a plan: a written transcript contract between the two apps, and an offline validator that fails loudly on a bad hand-off.

Why several states shrank. A source-supports-claim check re-reads every cited source and asks whether it actually supports the stance we wrote. It has now swept the map — Utah fell from 1,358 stances to 531 and out of the Deep tier — and a fabricated-source retirement closed inside it: 298 stances retired, 160 cited URLs denylisted, one cluster sourced to a newspaper that does not exist. Retirement is not the end state: a re-research worklist feeds those seats back through sourcing, and Oregon gained 38 stances in a cycle when the platform total fell.

How a stance gets seated. A member is placed on a topic because of a bill he sponsored, read in its session-law text — not a sentence someone else wrote about him. Three rules came out of the Washington sweep and now travel with every wave: a unanimous bill cannot evidence a position; cite the session law, not the introduced bill, because a bill can gain its substance during passage; and where a mechanism matches no chair clause by clause the answer is blank, not the closest chair. Blanks are recorded with a named reason — 49 in Washington, 7 more in Colorado Springs. And a blank is only as strong as the search behind it: Seattle’s ten “researched blanks” rested on a single source class, and re-research seated 48 rows across 11 of 11 offices.

Two corrections to this table, both worth keeping. Per-state counts once walked a single database link and so missed everyone seeded after occupancy moved onto dated office_terms: Wisconsin read 5 researched officials where it holds 208. Reading all three jurisdiction links fixed that and over-corrected in the same motion, sweeping in names discovered in campaign-finance filings that sit on placeholder offices with no geography. Migration 1702 settles it structurally — a real seat carries geography, a placeholder carries none. The durable lesson: the sum check this table relies on could not see either error, because a misattribution moves a row between buckets without changing the total.

Budgets you can follow

Treasury Tracker's database now holds budget data for 2,458 government entities across all 50 states plus DC, spanning fiscal years 1976 to 2026:

  • 2,016 cities, 338 counties, 50 states, 34 towns, and the federal budget
  • 2.3 million budget line items and 1.3 million individual transactions
  • 282,457 public salary records
  • State general funds upgraded to audited ACFR sources across all 50 states, each tie-verified to the penny; an independent blind re-derivation confirmed 149 of 151 exact matches, with the two exceptions documented
  • Los Angeles’ severed history is repaired — money in and money out are now 22 continuous years (FY2003–2024) of all-funds actuals from one source, with no unknown rows. The defect was a label: re-fetching revenue from the State Controller returned a figure dollar-identical to what sat under a portal label, in all four years tested. FY2025 and FY2026 were withdrawn rather than classified, the latter because it counted $4.8 billion of short-term borrowing alongside itself as spending — and an earlier verdict on the same data was retracted, because the tie that appeared to confirm it had been fitted rather than found
  • Every figure durably sourced. The core promise: no best-guess data wearing a real-looking label

Follow the money

The third leg of the transparency model, alongside votes and stances. The platform now holds 28.2 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $7.7 billion, covering 1,302 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing. The pipeline handles amended filings — when a committee refiles, superseded lines are retired individually instead of counted twice, which removed 18,553 duplicate rows and $20.2 million of phantom money — and the work has moved from backfilling to interrogating: a settled verdict on how refunds net against contributions, and a found mechanism for the negative Schedule A rows that made some committee totals look wrong. This is the “who funds whom” data behind Essentials, presented without commentary.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

Small, and not shallow

591 people used the platform in the last 90 days. That is the whole audience, across every app, over 1,874 sessions. What they do once they arrive is genuinely good — the average session runs just under eight minutes and 7 in 10 don’t bounce — so the constraint is arrival, not engagement.

The front door works, and this is the first pull comparable to the last one. 99 of the 306 people who reached the main site clicked through into an app — 32% in the quarter, 39% in the last 30 days. Last cycle changed the basis, counting each app once across every hostname that serves it after two apps turned out to be counted as several; re-running that hostname breakdown today surfaced no new host, so these figures rest on the same basis as the last briefing and can be read against it. The main site went 299 to 306. It gets re-run every cycle regardless: a new hostname is how the next undercount arrives.

Read every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a trend line. At this volume a couple of people either side is the entire swing. No work this cycle was aimed at traffic.

A number this page published, withdrawn — and the fix

An earlier version of this section said seven people in a quarter opened any of Treasury Tracker, as 5% of 152 visitors. The rate was not sound and it is withdrawn. The events were fine; the denominator was not. Treasury pushed its in-app navigation into the URL but never captured a pageview for it, so every visitor was recorded against the bare domain and nothing else — and the app opens on an entity picker rather than a budget. The direction was wrong too: those few produced 56 drills, 29 line-item views, 41 entity selections and 13 year changes — the handful who reach a budget use it hard.

The fix shipped on the 16th and is capturing. Budget views now arrive as their own URLs — ?entity=austin-tx&year=2025 and the like — and 10 of the 13 people on those hosts in the five days since reached one. Still not a publishable rate, and the sample says why: the entities in it are Austin, Travis County, Los Angeles and Colorado Springs — mostly us checking this cycle’s own onboarding. Treasury stays unmeasured rather than low until traffic that is not ours accumulates, but it is now measurable, which it was not when this card was written. The rule it bought applies to every app here: a low count is a claim about instrumentation until you have read the code path.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Run the city build again on the next served community, close the last 16 uncovered Washington legislators, and re-run Colorado’s legislative half once the state exposes more than the current session. Then: finish the 2026 House candidate waves, keep re-sourcing what the source-supports check retired, land Read & Rank candidate quotes, and get Fallacy Finders v1.1 real clips flowing through On the Record. Success in November looks like: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model actually lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not yet built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars to stand under it. We are honest about this distance; it is the point of the whole journey.
