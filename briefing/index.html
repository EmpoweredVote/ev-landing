The current push: 1,645 rows of city research, and not one of them published

Six cities in three states were researched end to end in four days — Charlotte; Bradenton, Miami and Tallahassee; Duluth and Saint Paul. Forty-seven seated members, thirty-five topics each, 1,645 researched rows. Thirty-seven of those rows propose an actual position, and every one of the thirty-seven is in front of a human being rather than in front of a voter. Nothing in this programme publishes itself, and nothing has: the stance total in the band above has not moved since September 27, and that is the honest reading of the week. We would rather report a research week as a research week than let a headline number drift upward on work nobody has checked.

Nearly half of the work is proof that a city is not allowed to act. 792 of the 1,645 rows are scope blanks, because a state can reserve a subject to itself and most do. That is a finding, not a failure — and it is why Minnesota was chosen third, to test the opposite case. It held: four ladders came alive there that neither Carolina nor Florida could reach. The detail is below.

The largest finding of the week was an evidence channel nobody had used. A measure that passes unanimously, by voice, records no division — so the vote says nothing about who wanted it, and our tools read that silence as absence. Asking instead who wrote it seated four Duluth members at once, off one resolution. Three tools were blind to it at the same moment: the divided-vote scan, the member vote lists, and every news sweep we run — not one article in any of the nine press corpora names a sponsor. When a measure passes without division, stop asking how people voted and ask who wrote it.

And one slice of our research was certified reliable by measurement rather than by assertion. Three AI coders read the same 57 state-level, record-based stances against human labels written blind, and agreed closely enough to clear the bar — with no severe disagreements at all. The same 57 items had failed that test before the rulebook was written. Figures below.

Three readers, one rulebook, and the first stratum we can call reliable

The Compass places a politician on a ladder from 1 to 5. Until now, the claim that those placements are consistent rested on the people making them. It is now measured. Three AI coders read the same 57 state-level stances that rest on a legislative record, and their answers were compared with human labels written at a blind desk, where the labeller never sees what any model said. The two agreement statistics came back at 0.830 and 0.932, the third test at 0.904, and the count of severe disagreements — two readers more than one rung apart — at zero. That clears the bar; the stratum was certified on October 3 by volunteer Chris Andrews.

Two controls are why this is worth printing. Eight of the 57 items had themselves been used as examples while the rulebook was written, which is a quiet way to grade your own homework; removing them leaves 54 items at 0.821 and 0.928, still certified. And the identical 57 items, coded before five rulings were added, scored 0.741 and did not pass — so the gain belongs to the rules, not the models, because the rulebook is the only thing that changed. One stratum of one evidence class is certified, and that is all. News-sourced and statement-sourced evidence are not, nor is the local tier the city work above lives in.

What it takes to say where a city councillor stands

A city is not a small state, and the first question is never what someone believes. It is whether the body they sit on may decide the thing at all. North Carolina and Florida reserve firearms, municipal wages and rent regulation to themselves, so 522 of those two tranches’ 1,015 rows are proof of a limit rather than a position. Minnesota reserves fewer — Saint Paul has a rent stabilisation ordinance, a municipal minimum wage, a chapter limiting city help with federal immigration enforcement, and ranked voting. But it reserves cannabis, which Florida does not, so a finding copied from one state to the next would have been wrong in both directions. The scope review runs before any research and is itself cited, to the statute and the municipal code chapter.

Then the sources have to be able to carry the claim, and ours sometimes could not. Three Saint Paul members were cited to one page: a meeting agenda that mentions two of them zero times and carries no roll call at all. The citation checker was right to refuse all three. The page that could carry the claim was one link away, and all three rows now verify on three sources of three with no value and no reasoning changed. When a citation fails, ask first whether the page could ever have carried the claim.

And a thin result is a claim about your tools until you have checked them. Re-mining the same cities with a link extractor that assumes no one publisher’s layout took Charlotte from three seated rows to five, Florida from none to two, and Florida’s attributed passages from 22 to 122. One member’s own press sweep returned 700 files of which nine named him, his surname being a common one; every passage finally used came from other members’ corpora. Price the next city off the second set of numbers.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

Small, and not shallow

673 people used the platform in the last 90 days, across every app, over 1,644 sessions. What they do once they arrive is good — sessions average six minutes sixteen and 7 in 10 don’t bounce — so the constraint is arrival, not engagement. 105 of the 337 who reached the main site clicked through into an app: 31% in the quarter, the third pull running at that share and each on a larger base.

Read every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a trend line. At this volume a couple of people either side is the whole swing, and the window slides, so an app can “fall” because a busy week in July rolled out of it. Over seven pulls the main site reads 306, 316, 312, 297, 300, 314, 337. The hostname map was re-run first and found no new host, which is what makes this pull readable against the last six.

A number this page published, withdrew — and what replaced it

An earlier edition said seven people in a quarter opened any of Treasury Tracker, as 5% of 152 visitors. That rate was not sound and it stays withdrawn. The events were fine; the denominator was not — Treasury pushed its in-app navigation into the address bar without recording a page view, so every visitor was filed against the bare domain. The direction was wrong too: this quarter those few produced 133 category drills, 93 entity selections and 71 line-item views.

The fix has recorded since August 16, and the two ways of counting still disagree by more than four times. 72 people reached a budget address; only 17 fired any past-the-front-page event. The split explains it: 55 landed on our own budget, one click from the donate page — a transparency visit, which selects nothing and so fires nothing. That leaves 19 on some other government’s budget, and that half moved for the first time in three pulls, 16 to 19, while the donate half went 47 to 55. Treasury stays unmeasured rather than low until the half that is not ours outgrows the donate traffic. A low count is a claim about instrumentation until you have read the code path.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Review the 37. A proposed position nobody has approved helps no voter, and the city programme makes more of them every week it runs: the review queue is the bottleneck now, not the research. Then the state legislators, where the evidence per row is far better than in any city — 2,551 seated people across 16 legislatures are in scope. Finish the 2026 candidate waves, write the one trivia collection an audit cannot fix, and time the Treasury page properly. Success in November is one thing: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0 — whose shell, shared theme, banner library and dashboard are groundwork done early. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars.
