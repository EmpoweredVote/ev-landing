The current push: three states, seat by seat

Pennsylvania, South Carolina and Minnesota were seated end to end in eleven days. 823 new officials, every one on a dated term: the Pennsylvania General Assembly at 253 of 253 with 252 portraits, plus Philadelphia, State College, Centre County and twenty county and row offices; the South Carolina General Assembly at 170 of 170 with a full portrait set, plus Columbia, Myrtle Beach and two counties; and the Minnesota Legislature at 201 seats, plus Duluth, Saint Paul and two county boards. Charlotte, Mecklenburg County and Boulder landed alongside them. Each seat was read one at a time against the government’s own roster, which is why these passes keep finding what a bulk import swallows: one portrait had been skipped on a note saying its source site published no usage terms, and the site publishes them, at an address answering the whole time. Nobody had asked it.

Tennessee went from one researched official to 115. A statewide ranked-choice-voting ban seated 74 legislators verbatim, and the Nashville Metro Council was read member by member out of the city’s own legislative record — 40 of 42 seats, both remaining zeros written down as zeros. Maryland moved too: its Cannabis Reform Act seated 116 members on a topic that had been empty. Total sourced stances went 35,877 to 36,420.

And one research pass was found filed under the wrong question. A Transportation Priorities pass on 82 Maine House members had been written into the Residential Zoning slot, which put members who voted to keep parking mandates on screen as broad upzoners. It survived review because one phrase — “reduce parking requirements” — appears on both ladders. The correction is written forward, not erased: the zoning rows are blanked rather than deleted, because deleting them would have let the old answer show through from the previous season. Maine’s stance count rose by 164 and none of it is new coverage. It is the same research, finally filed under the question it answers.

Platform Core & Infrastructure

The shared spine every app plugs into.

ev-accounts v2.23

Auth, tiers, XP and gems, politician database, TIGER geofencing, elections. This cycle it wrote its privacy rules down instead of holding them in people’s heads. A decision record sets the privacy floors — what the platform may show about a person, and when — with a retention policy for the stance audit, so a read of someone’s answers is bounded by a rule anybody can look up rather than by whoever wrote the endpoint. The audit’s pre-flight checks ran before it was built and found the first stage of the design wrong, which is what a pre-flight is for. Maryland’s state-legislative district identifiers had collapsed onto each other — 84 rows, not the 24 first reported — and the repair carries a guard so the shape cannot regress quietly. 9,189 districts mapped, 121,178 legislative votes and 19,622 bills on record.

On the Record

Speaker-attributed, searchable transcripts of public meetings, synced to the original video. It restarted. After five cycles of near-silence and two consecutive unexplained drops in the segment count, ten meetings landed — legislative days, a city council regular session, a full interview, and an LA mayoral debate — and the dataset now reads 164 meetings, 18,923 transcript segments and 1,190 identified speakers, including LA City Council with 7,200 recorded council votes. Two things to be straight about. The segment total is now above where it stood before the drops, so the new ingest more than covered them — but the drops themselves are still unexplained, and a total moving up does not answer why it moved down. And segments carry no creation timestamp, so the ten dated meetings are the evidence here, not the segment count. Built by volunteer Chris Andrews; it feeds the meetings system and the clip pipeline Fallacy Finders is waiting on.

A check that failed for three weeks, into a void

Treasury Tracker holds a set of frozen figures: budget numbers that have been verified and may not move without a recorded decision. A weekly invariant compares the live database against that set. It failed on September 7, 14 and 21 — three weeks running — and nobody was told. The job fired perfectly every time and wrote each verdict into a table nothing in the codebase reads, and the workflow that would have raised an issue had been switched off for want of a credential. The check worked. It reported into a void.

The data was never wrong. The database held an expectation three revisions out of date while the repository had moved on through decisions recorded properly each time; the differences match those notes exactly, which makes the diagnosis certain rather than plausible. The real residual was two rows, not sixty-five. Three things changed: the verdict is now read on every daily run, and a missing or stale one counts as a failure rather than a pass; the runner no longer reports one condition when it has found another; and a live privilege sweep now runs inside the database weekly, because the old guard read the text of code changes and could never see anything made through a console. We would rather publish this than a clean week. A monitor nobody reads is worse than no monitor, because it costs you the belief that you are being watched.

The table reconciles, and that is a weaker check than it looks. Every politician with at least one answer lands in exactly one row, and the rows sum exactly to the headline totals. That catches a missing row. It cannot catch a row filed in the wrong place, because a misattribution moves a count between buckets without changing the sum — which is how a past error survived a table that reconciled perfectly. The corroborating signal is agreement across cycles: rebuilt today, thirteen of the fourteen named rows reproduced their politician counts exactly. Tennessee is why the remainder row grew by 118 people, and it has no row of its own yet; next cycle it should.

How a stance gets seated. A member is placed on a topic because of a bill they sponsored, read in its session-law text — not a sentence someone else wrote about them. Three rules travel with every wave: a unanimous bill cannot evidence a position; cite the session law, not the introduced bill, because a bill can be gutted by a committee substitute long after members file it and a sponsor list never changes; and where a mechanism matches no rung clause by clause, the answer is blank. Two blanks this cycle are worth more than most of the seatings: one Nashville member’s record was read in full and yielded zero, and Tennessee’s eight education topics were triaged as unreachable from state statute.

Budgets you can follow

Treasury Tracker’s database holds budget data for 8,144 government entities across all 50 states plus DC, spanning fiscal years 1976 to 2028:

  • 2,903 cities, 2,787 townships, 949 boroughs, 699 counties, 486 towns, 253 villages, 50 states, and 17 others including the federal budget
  • That first line is a correction. Until today this page listed four classes — cities, counties, states and towns — and printed 3,502 of 7,507 entities as though that were the roster. The missing 4,005 were townships, boroughs and villages, which exist because a state hands over its governments in the classes its own publisher uses. The list now sums to the headline, and the refresh script complains if it ever stops
  • 2.7 million line items, 1.3 million transactions and 282,457 public salary records
  • State general funds upgraded to audited sources across all 50 states, each tie-verified to the penny; a blind re-derivation confirmed 149 of 151 exact matches, the two exceptions documented
  • A retired budget address now resolves through an alias instead of guessing, closing the defect that set the rule: an unrecognised slug used to resolve quietly to a real budget for the wrong city, which is worse than an honest “not found”
  • The core promise: no best-guess data wearing a real-looking label

Our own books, in our own tool

Empowered Vote’s budget is published in Treasury Tracker like any other entity, and putting ourselves through our own product found it describing us wrongly. The line under every hero headline read “Explore how public funds are allocated and spent” — including on our page, where the money is donations, not public funds. No figure was wrong, which is why nothing would have flagged it. The page now says what it is, and deliberately makes no completeness claim: it publishes the bank balance, and we also hold an untransferred wallet balance, so “every dollar” would be an overstatement. A transparency page is the worst place to overstate coverage.

Follow the money

The third leg, alongside votes and stances. The platform holds 28.2 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $7.8 billion, covering 1,302 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing. The pipeline handles amended filings — superseded lines are retired individually instead of counted twice. This is the “who funds whom” data behind Essentials, presented without commentary.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

Small, and not shallow

609 people used the platform in the last 90 days, across every app, over 1,703 sessions. What they do once they arrive is good — sessions average six and a half minutes and 7 in 10 don’t bounce — so the constraint is arrival, not engagement. 93 of the 300 who reached the main site clicked through into an app, 31% in the quarter.

Read every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a trend line. At this volume a couple of people either side is the entire swing, and the quarter window slides forward, so an app can “fall” because a busy week in June rolled out of it. Nothing this cycle was aimed at arrival, so 635 to 609 is the expected result of not working on it. The hostname map was re-run before any of these figures were pulled and found no new host, which is what makes this pull readable against the last four: the main site went 306, 316, 312, 297, 300. One host that served nobody last quarter served somebody this one — the argument for leaving quiet hosts in the filter rather than tidying them out.

A number this page published, withdrew — and what replaced it

An earlier edition said seven people in a quarter opened any of Treasury Tracker, as 5% of 152 visitors. That rate was not sound and it stays withdrawn. The events were fine; the denominator was not — Treasury pushed its in-app navigation into the address bar without recording a page view, so every visitor was filed against the bare domain. The direction was wrong too: this quarter those few produced 110 category drills, 82 entity selections and 61 line-item views. The handful who reach a budget use it hard.

The fix has been recording since August 16, and the two ways of counting still disagree by five times. 52 people reached a budget address; only 11 fired any past-the-front-page event. The split explains it: 41 of the 52 landed on our own budget, one click from the donate page — a transparency visit, not budget exploration, and it selects nothing, so it fires nothing. That leaves 12 who opened some other government’s budget, down from 16. Neither is published as a rate. Treasury stays unmeasured rather than low until the half that is not ours outgrows the donate traffic. The rule it bought applies to every app here: a low count is a claim about instrumentation until you have read the code path.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Research the seats just loaded — Pennsylvania, South Carolina and Minnesota have rosters and almost no stances. Grade the budget rows still marked unknown. Finish the 2026 House candidate waves and land Read & Rank candidate quotes once the pool can carry them. Success in November looks like one thing: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0 — whose shell, shared theme and sibling-shard view are groundwork done early. The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars.
