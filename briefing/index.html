The current push: ready for November

Since June 7, nearly all effort has gone into the Inform pillar, on purpose. The 2026 midterms are under three months out, and the work that moved is exactly what an anonymous voter touches in an election year: House candidate coverage that now spans the whole country (over 1,600 2026 candidates carry researched stances, from the biggest states down to the single-district delegations, with the enacted 2026 district maps now imported for the largest states), audited state budget pipelines that reached all 50 states, campaign-finance records that trace the money behind every federal candidate, city-by-city official seeding, and a fast-growing public-meetings transcript system. The Connect pillar apps are intentionally parked until after this push; that is sequencing, not abandonment.

Since the last briefing the whole platform pointed at one state. Washington had five researched officials two briefings ago and has 158 today, and it is the first state built on every layer at once: TIGER places and legislative districts loaded, the Legislature structured into 2 chambers and 147 offices, all 147 members seated on dated terms with official portraits, the nine statewide executives seeded behind them, then King County (its own government, 9 council geofences, 14 offices, its 2026 county races) and Seattle (7 council geofences, 11 offices, the District 5 race), and two days later Kitsap County and Bainbridge Island (16 electeds on dated terms, Kitsap's seven 2026 ballot offices, both routed by address). Treasury Tracker followed the same map — Seattle and King County, then Bainbridge Island and Kitsap County, then Tacoma and Spokane — every row taken from the State Auditor's bound audited statements, so the money carries audit-attested provenance rather than a press release's. Essentials paints Seattle as a served chip and King County as a county entry, each with a banner of its own, and Bainbridge Island and Kitsap County now sit beside them — the city as a second chip, the county as a searchable entry. Washington entered the Deep tier on the map below three days after it was one of the palest tiles on it.

The Washington stances were seated a new way, and the method is the story. A member is placed on a topic because of a bill he sponsored, read in its session-law text — not because of a sentence someone else wrote about him. 131 of the 147 legislators now carry at least one sourced position, and the 49 documented blanks carry something nearly as useful: a named reason no instrument on that ladder reaches a chair. Three rules came out of it and are now written down. A unanimous bill cannot evidence a position — the highest-reach candidate instrument was thrown out after the roll call read 48–0 and 96–0, because chairs describe a preference among competing values and unanimity demonstrates none. Cite the session law, not the introduced bill, because ESHB 2015 gained its co-response personnel during passage, and a cohort seated from the introduced version would have been reasoning about a different act. And where a purpose-free mechanism matches no chair clause by clause, the answer is blank, not the chair that reads closest. That last rule arrived as a reversal: a migration extended an earlier ruling one step too far, seated twelve senators, flagged itself for review, and was reversed the same evening. Coverage went backwards, 136 to 131. The sweep also found a defect in our own ladders rather than in the data — one enacted act funds both more officers and more co-response, a combination two chairs forbid by their wording, so fourteen people sit at a chair whose text they would only half recognise. It is logged as a trouble spot rather than smoothed over. Then the method was run the other way, and it overturned a claim this page had already published. Seattle's eleven city offices were reported as ten researched blanks; that rested on a single source class — Legistar roll calls and ordinance PDFs — reported in the vocabulary of exhaustive research. Re-run against web sources, led by candidate questionnaires, it seated 48 rows across all 11 of 11 Seattle offices, and King County went from 6 covered of 14 to 11 of 14 at 35 rows. The rule now written down: instrument-first research proves what an institution did, person-first research finds what a person says — and a blank is only as strong as the search behind it.

Meanwhile the stance audit spent the cycle putting rows back. Todd Gloria — last briefing's headline error, the first openly gay mayor of San Diego stored at “make same-sex marriage illegal” — is re-seated from the city's own records. Berkeley and Maryland were re-read the same way, chairs evidenced from council records and roll calls: across five migrations, 8 officials re-seated, 2 restored and 14 rows re-sourced, with the rest blanked where the record genuinely does not support a position. The largest single correction was structural: 117 rows on the judicial ladders had been answered by people who are not judges, and were blanked. An orphan-context regression went 224 down to 50, and CI now refuses any migration that deletes an answer without declaring what happens to its reasoning, with the stance-sourcing check moved into a job of its own so nothing can mask it. The stance total rose for the first time in five briefings — and it rose while all of that was being blanked, because Washington added more than the audit removed. The headshot backlog fell from 1,545 to 1,118 by the 15th, most of it Washington taken end to end at 181 portraits of 181 seats across the state, King County and Seattle; 57 of those still missing sit on live profile pages carrying researched stances. The 16 Kitsap and Bainbridge seats seeded on the 16th have no portraits yet, and they are the only seats added since that do not.

Platform Core & Infrastructure

The shared spine every app plugs into.

ev-accounts v2.22

Auth, tiers, XP and gems, politician database, TIGER geofencing, elections. This cycle the core carried Washington end to end — geography, chambers, offices, dated terms, portraits and 2026 races for the state, King County and Seattle, then Kitsap County and Bainbridge Island — on top of the stance sweep described above. Three guards went in with it: CI now refuses a migration that deletes an answer without declaring what happens to its reasoning, the stance-sourcing check runs in a job of its own so nothing can mask it, and a boundary load that leaves a child→county link stale now warns loudly instead of silently. Standing from earlier cycles: 2026 House candidate coverage in every state; every California county seeded (58 of 58); every California and Oregon official web URL repointed or documented; CI detection of migration-number collisions; a dedup pass that refuses to merge name collisions that are provably different people, the failure mode that had already produced one fabricated mayor; and the occupancy-evidence view that answers “seated official or discovered name?” in one column, structurally rather than by provenance. The enacted 2026 district polygons are imported for TX, FL, NC, CA and OH; Wisconsin, Bend OR and Collin County TX were stood up the same way Washington was. Underneath all of it, ADR 0002 moved officeholder occupancy onto dated office_terms, so who holds a seat is a fact with a start and end date rather than a flag. 7,346 districts mapped, 121,178 legislative votes and 19,622 bills on record.

On the Record

Speaker-attributed, searchable transcripts of public meetings, synced to the original video. Live in the pipeline: 153 meetings, 17,660 transcript segments, 946 identified speakers, including LA City Council with 7,200 recorded council votes. Built by volunteer Chris Andrews; it feeds the platform's meetings system. It was the fastest-growing dataset on the platform through July, and it has now held flat for two cycles — no ingest run while the stance work had the floor. Reported plainly, because a growth claim that stops being true is exactly the kind of number this page exists to correct. The Fallacy Finders hand-off is no longer a plan: a written transcript contract between the two apps, and an offline validator that fails loudly on a bad hand-off, proven against a real transcript.

Why several states shrank. A source-supports-claim check re-reads every cited source and asks whether it actually supports the stance we wrote. Pointed at our own best-researched coverage first, it has now swept the map, and the bill came due over four briefings: Oregon lost 145 rows, Utah fell from 1,358 stances to 531 and out of the Deep tier, then California, the national set, and Maryland — down 258 with several hundred more repaired or re-sourced. A fabricated-source retirement closed inside that: 298 stances retired and 160 cited URLs denylisted, including a cluster sourced to a newspaper that does not exist. Newton, Massachusetts was the starkest single case — a clean roster, and 48 of its 55 stances citing pages that had never existed. Retirement is not the end state. A re-research worklist feeds the removed seats back through sourcing: 18 bad Bend rows became 45 verified ones, Somerville got 32 of 62 back on named roll calls, and Oregon gained 38 stances in a cycle when the platform total fell. Two cities returned nothing, which is also a finding — Carson yields 3 of 34 because its Legistar record is stale, and Lynn 0 of 30 because the city publishes no council record at all.

Two corrections to this table, both worth keeping. Per-state counts used to walk a single database link, and so missed everyone seeded after a schema change moved officeholder occupancy onto dated office_terms: Wisconsin read 5 researched officials where it holds 208, and its stances were filed under “2026 candidates”. Reading all three jurisdiction links fixed that and over-corrected in the same motion, sweeping in names discovered in campaign-finance filings that sit on placeholder offices with no geography — Indiana then read 27 where it holds 20. Migration 1702 settles it structurally: a real seat carries geography, a placeholder carries none. Both rules now run together, because they pull in opposite directions. The lesson underneath is the durable one — the sum check this table relies on could not see either error. It reconciled perfectly on both wrong versions, because a misattribution moves a row between buckets without changing the total. It catches missing rows, not misplaced ones. Per-state figures in briefings before August 12 undercount the term-linked states and should not be compared row-for-row against these.

  • 2.3 million budget line items and 1.3 million individual transactions

  • Every figure durably sourced. The core promise: no best-guess data wearing a real-looking label

Follow the money

The third leg of the transparency model, alongside votes and stances. The platform now holds 27.6 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $7.6 billion, covering 1,302 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing. The pipeline handles amended filings — when a committee refiles, superseded lines are retired individually instead of counted twice, which removed 18,553 duplicate rows and $20.2 million of phantom money — and the work has moved from backfilling to interrogating: a local DuckDB mirror for fast exploration, a settled verdict on how refunds net against contributions, and a found mechanism for the negative Schedule A rows that made some committee totals look wrong. This is the “who funds whom” data behind Essentials, presented without commentary so users draw their own conclusions.

Reach & Engagement

Who actually uses what we build — measured, not guessed.

Small, and not shallow

535 people used the platform in the last 90 days. That is the whole audience, across every app. What they do once they arrive is genuinely good — the average session runs just under eight minutes and 7 in 10 don't bounce — so the constraint is arrival, not engagement, and it now says so measured across the whole ecosystem rather than inferred from one app.

A claim from the last briefing does not survive this pull. We wrote that the gap was the hand-off from the main site into the tools. It isn't: 92 of the 252 people who landed on empowered.vote clicked through into an app — 37% in the quarter, 41% in the last 30 days. The front door works. What varies, and varies enormously, is what happens on the other side of it, which is what the two cards below now measure.

Read every figure here as an order of magnitude, not a trend line. At this volume a couple of people either side is the entire swing, and it is always tempting to explain a move that is really just noise. No work this cycle was aimed at traffic.

Treasury Tracker is the second most-visited thing we run and holds the largest dataset on the platform — 2.3 million budget line items. Whether those 152 people reached a budget is the one thing this page cannot tell you; see the note below.

A number this page published yesterday, withdrawn

Yesterday's version of this section said seven people in a quarter opened any of Treasury Tracker, as 5% of 152 visitors, and called it the sharpest finding on the page. The rate was not sound and it is withdrawn. The events are fine — clicking a department in the icicle chart to go a level deeper is counted, because the drill event is driven off the chart's own navigation state and covers the icicle, the sunburst and the category list alike. The denominator is the problem. Treasury pushes its in-app navigation into the URL but never captures a pageview for it, so every one of those 152 visitors is recorded against the bare domain and nothing else. Essentials, Read & Rank and Civic Trivia do capture their route changes, which is why their rates above are comparable and this one is not. The app also opens on an entity picker rather than on a budget, so an unknown share of those 152 never had a chart in front of them to drill. Comparing “everyone who loaded the domain” against “people who clicked inside a budget” reads as a verdict on the product when it is mostly a verdict on our instrumentation.

And the direction was wrong too. “Seven people” is not seven glances: those few produced 56 drills into the chart, 29 line-item views, 41 entity selections and 13 year changes — about fourteen drills each. The handful who reach a budget use it hard. What we cannot yet say is how many people reached one at all. The fix is a pageview on in-app navigation, which every other app on this list already sends; until it ships, Treasury's engagement rate is unmeasured rather than low, and this page will say unmeasured.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Close the last 16 uncovered Washington legislators and fan the same build out to the rest of the state (WA-CITIES-01 in Treasury, more served communities in Essentials), finish the 2026 House candidate waves, keep re-sourcing what the source-supports check retired, land Read & Rank candidate quotes, and get Fallacy Finders v1.1 real clips flowing through On the Record. Success in November looks like: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model actually lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not yet built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars to stand under it. We are honest about this distance; it is the point of the whole journey.
