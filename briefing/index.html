The November test, re-scored

We were wrong about where the results lived, and finding them cut our score in half. The Associated Press publishes state legislative races in a per-state file we had never read. It holds 5,759 of them and 5,439 are already called. We recorded 403, in the nine states where we hold candidate rows. The correction below has the detail.

So the ballot test looks much worse, and that is the honest reading. 1,093 of the 2,340 races now have a winner, against 508 last time, and they returned 1,134 winners. We hold a readable position for 767, a portrait for 913 and campaign-finance records for 454. 432 have all three and 217 have none. Four days ago the same test read 403 of 508. Corrections

What this edition fixes in the one before it. A published edition is never edited; the next one says what it corrects.

We said the Associated Press does not call state legislative races. It does. The last edition found no result for any of the 1,114 of them, and said the returns would have to come from state officials one state at a time. The count was true of our data. The explanation was wrong. AP's national results file carries nine kinds of office and no legislative contest, which is where that belief came from. AP also publishes a file per state, and those 51 files hold 6,938 races, of which 5,759 are state legislative and 5,439 already called. The catalogue we read was a property of one endpoint, not of the source.

Not every result on this page comes from the Associated Press any more. The last edition said every called race was an AP call. Of the 1,134 winners we now hold, 936 come from AP, 114 from Ballotpedia and 84 from an election official's own published results. All 1,134 are recorded as called rather than certified, and our records carry that distinction on each row.

The ballot figures are on a changed basis for the second cycle running. The last edition counted 2,235 races and 4,116 people in the 53 November 3 elections. The same elections now hold 2,340 races and 4,355 people, after statewide board and Los Angeles city seats were added. The two editions do not compare row for row.

Treasury's action count is not stuck at 31; it fell to 30. The last edition reported 31 for a third edition running, as though it were frozen. It is a 90-day rolling window, so a person whose only visit was 91 days ago drops out without anything changing at Treasury. Arrivals rose from 325 to 349.

One sweep was running while we counted, and it was not a load. A campaign-finance pass has been rewriting existing contribution rows since November 8, 53,511 of them on Monday and 55,725 today. We counted the table twice, five minutes apart, and both reads returned 29,319,606 rows. Nothing else was moving.

Nobody had ever asked whether the link under the chart still works

Every gate Treasury Tracker runs reads money or identity: the tie gate, the per-capita guard, the frozen invariant. Not one of them reads a URL. So in October a federal clearinghouse answered with a link that expired in thirty seconds, and 227 Alaska rows shipped a citation that died before the load finished. Every figure on those pages was right. The only broken thing was the proof.

The new gate asks the live web about all 1,857 distinct citations, covering 560,684 budgets. The first run was red and honestly so: 1,689 live, 162 dead across 20 hosts, 6 that could not be asked, and 7,142 budgets citing a dead link. It refuses two shortcuts. An unanswered host fails unless a person has declared the outage and dated it. And a 200 is not proof, because a block page answers 200 too.

Then the gate was wrong twice, in the same shape. Ohio's Auditor of State took its web server offline on October 25 after a cyberattack and is still down. Every path there answers 404, so the gate called 60 URLs dead and asked us to rewrite 6,616 correct citations during an outage. A publisher that is offline is not a document that is gone. Separately, three fleet runs reported 102, then 77, then 79 dead links. A backlog that will not hold still is measuring itself. That was rate-limit replies and hosts that refuse automated clients, filed as if the publisher had spoken.

South Carolina was the real thing, and it was repaired. All 24 of its citations were dead because the Comptroller General moved to a new site. The first pattern we derived was wrong and looked right: it resolved for the one year we tested, and would have replaced 46 dead links with 46 different ones. So every replacement was probed alone, and every file read back to confirm its year and state.

The draft season is excluded because the service a voter reads excludes it. We checked that from outside the database, on four people chosen so the two bases would disagree. Two whose only answers are draft returned 0. Two researched weeks ago returned 12 and 10, matching us to the row. The live topic list returned 60, which is the open season. The ballot figures come from the 53 elections dated November 3 and the 2,340 races inside them. A person counts as covered on three separate tests: a published-season answer that is not a withdrawal, a default portrait, and a campaign-finance filing identity that links to them. Every result is recorded as called and none as certified. 936 of the 1,134 winners come from the Associated Press, 114 from Ballotpedia and 84 from an election official. The coverage table reconciles against the stored row count printed beside it, and our refresh script checks that table against the map bucket by bucket. Budget grades are read from the budget rows themselves and the six sum to the total. Reach figures are the 30 and 90 days ending November 10, 2026, test traffic excluded, each app counted once across every hostname serving it.
