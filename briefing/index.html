A kind of government we had never held before, and 717 of them at once

This is a three-day cycle, and almost all of it is Treasury. 8,925 governments now have budget data, up 778 — 61 New York cities outside New York City, and 717 New York school districts covering fiscal years 1995 to 2025. The school districts alone carry 82,564 budgets and 2.4 million line items, which is why the line-item total went from 2.7 million to 5.3 million over a weekend. A school district is not a city, a county or a state; it is a single-purpose government that taxes and spends, and until this weekend this platform held none.

Nothing announced them. A catch-all bucket did. The entity breakdown further down this page names seven classes and sweeps everything else into an “other” row that is computed, never typed. That row went from 17 to 734, and reading it is the only reason anyone knew a new class of government had arrived. This is the second time that bucket has earned its keep: it was added in September after a four-class list printed 3,502 of 7,507 entities as though it were the whole roster. A breakdown that cannot surprise you is not measuring anything.

The other half of the report is that the stance line stopped dead. Not one row has been written to the stance table since Friday the 9th — the headline above is the same 32,000 it was then, to the position. Last edition closed a review queue and called it paid off once rather than solved; three days later nothing has refilled it. A flat number is a finding, and this one is the whole state of the research programme this cycle.

What a school district is doing in a platform about ballots

It is worth saying why 717 of these are a good use of a weekend, because they are not on anybody’s ballot in the way a mayor is. A New York school district levies its own tax, adopts its own budget, and in most of the state puts that budget to a public vote every May. It is one of the few governments an ordinary person is asked to approve a number for directly — and the number is, for most households, the largest local line on the bill. A platform that says it helps people follow the money cannot hold every city in New York and none of the districts that spend more of it.

What this does not do is make them comparable to a city, and the app does not yet know that. A district’s budget is mostly instructional salaries and state aid; a city’s is police, fire, roads and sanitation. The comparison gate already refuses to set a cash-basis budget beside a GAAP one, which is the same class of problem one layer down, and it has not been asked this question yet. Until it is, the honest framing is that we hold these budgets rather than that we compare them. That is named here so it is on the record before somebody charts one against the other.

Follow the money

The third leg, alongside votes and stances. The platform holds 29.1 million individual campaign-finance contributions totaling $8.6 billion, covering 2,260 federal politicians and candidates across 24 election cycles (1980 to 2026). Every record traces to its original FEC filing, and superseded lines from amended filings are retired individually rather than counted twice. This cycle’s work was subtraction: the California committee sweep described above removed roughly 970 wrong attachments and kept the several hundred that turned out to be real. A money page that credits a contribution to the wrong person is worse than one that is missing it, which is why that work counts as progress even though every figure in this paragraph either held still or barely moved.

724 people used the platform in the last 90 days, across every app, over 1,554 sessions and 3,741 page views. Treasury is still unmeasured rather than low — and this cycle the two counts were read properly

An earlier edition published a rate here — seven people in a quarter, as 5% of 152 visitors — and withdrew it. It stays withdrawn. The events were sound; the denominator was not, because Treasury pushes its in-app navigation into the address bar without recording a page view, so every visitor is filed against the bare domain. The direction stays wrong too: this quarter those few people produced 166 category drills, 101 entity selections, 101 line-item views, 56 year changes, 19 searches and 18 chart changes.

A note on how the other half is counted, because the published series is not quite what it looked like. Earlier editions reported it by subtraction — 82 minus our own — and printed 16, then 19, then 23. Asked directly, 25 people this quarter opened some other government’s budget; asked by subtraction the same window gives 22. The gap is three people who looked at both. Neither reading changes the conclusion: the donate-driven half is still more than twice the other, and the day that reverses is the day this section gets a real denominator. It is not that day.

The Road Ahead

How much further we have to go, honestly.

Now to Nov

Election readiness

Restart the stance work. It is the one thing on this list that did not happen this cycle, and with three weeks to the election it is the thing a voter will notice missing. The audit’s tail bands are untouched; 24 rungs still ask a different question in Season 2 than in Season 1 and need a ruling before anything carries across. Then put the 8.4 million votes to work — 3,861 legislators carry at least one recorded vote, and that record feeds exactly the evidence class the one certified stratum rests on. Finish the 2026 candidate waves, settle whether the trivia generator’s remaining slot-C share is design or defect, write the one collection an audit cannot fix, and time the Treasury page properly. Success in November is one thing: any voter in a served area can look up every name on their ballot and follow the money behind it.

Post-election

The Connect pivot

Reactivate the parked pillar: Empowered Listening phase 6, Focused Communities v1.1, Civic Spaces v4.0 — whose shell, shared theme, banner library and dashboard are groundwork done early, and which went deliberately quiet this cycle. This is where the platform stops being a reference tool and becomes a community, and where our sustaining-donor model lives.

The long game

The Empower pillar

Designed but not built: the Gems stipend economy, Awareness Exchange, Badges, Symposium tournaments, Emparks, Common Grounds, and tools for citizens to run for office on the strength of their solutions. Roughly a third of the founding vision, waiting on the first two pillars.
